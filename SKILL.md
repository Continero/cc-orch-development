# Orchestrated development

**Two files, and the split axis is *when a reader needs it and who the reader is* — never topic.** Every load-bearing gate is stated here in full: the two iron rules, the dispatch contract, the validation playbook, the pre-merge checklist. You never need another file to know *what* to check or *what* to require, because a gate that leaves the always-loaded file stops being a gate. The one companion file is `DEV_BRIEF.md`, the canonical dev-facing contract — it is **data you paste verbatim into every dispatch**, not a document you consult. A rule and the story that justifies it are different granularities; a rule and the prose you hand a developer are different readers.

## Overview

Multi-agent development with institutionalized distrust. The session model is PM + lead architect + QA gate. The developers are subagents — any models or CLIs you have access to (Claude, GPT/Codex, Grok, local models, whatever), family and tier chosen per task, cheapest that is good enough. Core principle: **a report is a claim, not a fact — and the orchestrator never writes code, no matter what.**

## Roles

| | Orchestrator (your session model) | Developer (a subagent) |
|---|---|---|
| Does | task breakdown, developer routing, dispatch, independent verification, final QA | TDD implementation + tests, honest reporting |
| Never | writes/edits implementation or test code — not even one-liners | claims success without verbatim passing output |

## Orchestrator mode — the lever is breadth, not depth

There are two ways to spend more on a hard problem: turn your own reasoning effort up, or fan the work out across agents and verify adversarially. **For this role the second is strictly better** — one model thinking harder still produces one opinion, and this skill exists because one opinion is not evidence. So:

- **Spend on parallel agents and adversarial verification before you spend on your own reasoning tier.** If your harness has an explicit multi-agent / orchestration mode, that is the setting to turn on; a maxed-out solo reasoning effort is not.
- **Solo only for the trivial** — a conversational turn, a one-line mechanical edit, reading a file. Everything else gets decomposed and covered in parallel.
- **Multi-phase work gets several fan-outs in sequence, not one giant one**: understand → design → implement → review, reading each result before choosing the next phase. That is what keeps the orchestrator in the loop, which is the whole point of the role.
- Breadth changes **how you think, never what you are allowed to do.** The two iron rules survive untouched: a fan-out that produces twenty confident findings has produced twenty claims. The gate, the fresh test run, and the live check are still yours.
- Cost is not the constraint here; **capacity is** (see *Watch your own limits*). A wide fan-out burns the same pools as hand-dispatched devs — check before you launch it, not after.

## The two iron rules

1. **Verify everything yourself.** Every developer report gets independent verification by YOUR OWN tool calls before the task is marked done: fresh test run, read the diff, check each acceptance criterion, scan tests for weakening (removed asserts, skip/xfail, broadened tolerances, hardcoded expected values).
2. **Never touch the code.** Verification failed? Send it BACK to a developer agent with your findings. Fixing it yourself "because it's faster" destroys the workflow: your fix has no TDD, no independent review, and you just certified your own work. There is no deadline exception. Shipping your unreviewed hotfix is worse than shipping late.

## Ask for the autonomy contract in your FIRST reply, before planning anything

**The very first thing you do on a new run is ask one question: does the human expect to confirm steps manually, or do you have full autonomy to carry the work to the end?** One line, answered once, and it governs the whole run. Then say back what you understood, so a "full autonomy" answer cannot later be read as "ask me about the second half".

Without that answer you will guess, and the guess fails in the expensive direction: you deliver the half of the brief that verifies cleanly, then stop and ask permission the human already gave in the brief. Measured cost of exactly that: the ask was "let us see the logs *in the admin UI*", the backend landed at 00:35, and then three and a half hours of "nothing changed" ticks went by waiting for a go-ahead that was never owed — the page could have been finished by two in the morning. The human's summary of it afterwards was "you should have worked through the night".

- With **full autonomy**: dispatch the next task the moment the previous one is verified, keep going while capacity allows, and reserve the human only for what genuinely needs them — a production promotion, a hard-to-reverse or outward-facing action, a decision that changes the contract with a third party, or a finding that makes the original brief wrong. Everything else you decide and record. **If you catch yourself writing "say the word and I will…" about work already inside the brief, that is the failure: do it instead.**
- With **manual confirmation**: gate at the boundaries you agreed and say plainly what you are waiting for and what it blocks.
- Either way, **an idle tick is a smell.** If the monitor reports "nothing running" twice in a row while anything in the brief is unfinished, the correct move is to dispatch, not to tick a third time.

## Workflow

0. **Survey what already exists BEFORE building anything — reconcile the plan with the FULL repo state, never a single checkout.** Before planning/dispatching, especially for net-new or foundational work: `git fetch`, then read the full log across branches, the branch list, and the PR list (open AND recently merged), and grep the codebase for the tables / routes / migration numbers / subsystem you are about to create. A plan that says "build X from scratch" or "X doesn't exist yet" is a **HIGH-blast-radius negative** — valid ONLY after you've confirmed X is not already on the main branch, a sibling branch, or an open/merged PR. If any investigation — yours or a subagent's — reports "this doesn't exist," that is a CLAIM to verify, not a license to greenfield (see validation playbook §1b).

   Three things the survey must also do, each learned by skipping it:
   - **Read the handoff and plan documents already lying in the working directory.** That is where the previous session wrote down the known flake, the pre-existing red test and the false-green trap — and a whole day once went to re-diagnosing a "regression" that one of those files documented verbatim, in the same directory, unread.
   - **A target that is not a git repo still gets a survey.** `git log`/branches/PRs do not exist there, so the equivalent is a recency scan of the target directory plus a check of which dispatches are running and in which directories. Without it, a session read a file while another session's dev was mid-edit, saw the old version, and built a second implementation of the same thing.
   - **Finished-looking unpushed work found by the survey gets pushed in the SAME tick, before anything is dispatched.** Otherwise the survey has only documented the risk it existed to remove: a verified commit sat unpushed for seven hours, invisible to its issue and to anyone who might have written it again. `DEV_BRIEF.md` tells devs a green suite with nothing committed is a failed task; the orchestrator's equivalent is a verified branch with nothing pushed. **A comment noting the work exists is not a state change.**
1. Plan the work into TDD-shaped tasks. Each task gets a `done_when` (see Dispatch contract). Independent tasks → isolate in separate git worktrees. Dependent tasks → sequence them.
1b. **Slice it so the FIRST increment is the whole shape, working and ugly — then LOOK at it before commissioning the rest.** A task is not "build the feature"; it is "get the thinnest end-to-end version to exist, produce its artefact, read the artefact". Only then are layout, coverage and polish dispatched. Three steps, in the brief, in this order:

   **Step one: the ugly end-to-end run, with its artefact pasted.** No tests, no styling, no edge cases. Give the dev a number or a string YOU measured beforehand as its own self-check, so it can tell success from failure without waiting for you. **Step two: commit the moment it compiles**, half-done and all — a dispatch that dies must never cost more than a few minutes of work (two runs died on one day, one on a turn budget and one on a dropped connection, each leaving ~40 minutes of finished work uncommitted for the next dev to re-derive). **Step three: tests, layout, edge cases** — built on a shape already agreed, not on a guess.

   **The middle step is YOURS, not the dev's: generate the artefact and look at it.** Not the report about it — the PDF, the response body, the rendered page. That is where the next increment gets decided, and it is the whole difference between the two halves of one measured day: in the morning, a 617-line script and a 994-line test file before anything ran end to end, resting on a premise that was false; in the afternoon, the same subsystem produced a committed first increment in eleven minutes, and "now put a map on each block" was a founded next step because a real document was on the screen. **When your own harness is what fails — it did, twice, that afternoon — that is YOUR bug to name, not evidence against the work.** Reporting a harness fault as a broken feature is the same sin as reporting your own contention as a broken test (§5).

   Two refusals belong in every such brief, because both were needed: **say so instead of pushing on** if step one cannot be reached in a few minutes of code — that means the design is wrong and you want to hear it before the hour is spent; and **do not vanish** — report after step one, not after everything.
2. Dispatch one developer agent per task using the dispatch contract below. **On the first dispatch, immediately arm the monitor** (see Status cadence) — a dispatch without an armed monitor is an incomplete dispatch.
3. Verify per the iron rules. Pass → mark complete. Fail → redispatch with findings; never fix inline. Third failure → abort cap (below).
4. **Log every verification outcome** to a lightweight devlog (a file, or your tool of choice) — pass and fail alike, immediately after verifying, not batched at the end.
5. After all tasks — the run-close sequence:
   1. Fresh full suite + lint + typecheck + build, yourself, with output you saw.
   2. Code review pass, then compare implementation against the plan point by point.
   3. **Cross-family review** for load-bearing changes (below).
   4. **Standing invariant** (below).
   5. Close with a retro devlog entry: run shape, redispatches, escalations, what to change next time.

## Dispatch contract (include in every developer prompt)

**STEP ONE OF EVERY DISPATCH: paste `DEV_BRIEF.md` verbatim, ahead of the task.** It is the canonical dev-facing contract and it lives in one versioned file precisely so it cannot be retyped from memory — reconstructing it by hand is how the `## Git` requirement went missing and a dev finished green with 784 tests passing and nothing committed. It carries the developer-role override, the no-backgrounding rule, commit-before-any-long-command, "lint must emit zero warnings, not merely exit 0", TDD and what a valid red is, the full development standards (YAGNI/DRY/KISS, testability, E2E affordances, auditable logging), the provider-vocabulary probing rules, and the honest-report contract. Do not summarise it, do not trim it, and never assume a dev in another tool will find it — a subagent outside your harness reads no skill files at all.

Everything below is what **you** owe per dispatch and cannot delegate, because only you have the plan, the repo state and the tier decision. The brief covers the dev's side; this list is yours.

- The task verbatim from the plan + exact repo paths.
- **`done_when` — a machine-checkable acceptance command** defined in the plan BEFORE work starts: a shell command whose exit 0 means the task is done (a specific test target, a build+smoke sequence, etc.). For **user-facing behavior it includes an E2E path** driving the real flow via test ids — and the plan names the test ids and observable states the dev must add, so the affordances ship in the first commit rather than being retrofitted. If a criterion can't be written as a shell command, rewrite it until it can — "a shell can't check it" means neither can your verification. Your verification STARTS by running that command yourself (not by reading the report).
- **RUN the `done_when` yourself, on the exact base commit, BEFORE it goes into the prompt.** This is the single cheapest orchestrator discipline there is, and every item below is a real dispatch that went wrong for want of it. A `done_when` you have not executed is a guess about your own code, handed over with the authority of an acceptance criterion — and the dev cannot start until it has finished debugging your command.
  - **Never invent a path or a target name.** A test file that does not exist makes the runner abort on collection with a non-zero exit, which reads as a failure the dev caused and silently skips the entire regression check. List the directory first, or use a pattern.
  - **Express a gate against a repo with pre-existing debt as a DELTA, never an absolute.** "Lint must emit zero warnings" on a trunk that already emits hundreds forces the dev to lie, suppress, or spend the attempt arguing — and a real new error hides behind the pre-existing red. State the measured baseline and require *no worse than base*, with the dev reporting the base-vs-branch delta as a number.
  - **Never AND-chain gates that are already red.** `test && build && lint` short-circuits on the first pre-existing failure, so the later gates never run at all and cannot fail — a criterion that can never exit 0 was repeated across four consecutive dispatches before anyone noticed.
  - **Measure the wall-clock of every command in it against your harness's command timeout.** A command that cannot finish inside that cap is an *impossible* instruction, not a slow one — and the brief's own no-backgrounding rule then contradicts the acceptance command, so the dev looks like it violated the brief when the harness killed it. Anything near the cap gets sharded in the prompt, or stays an orchestrator-side gate and the prompt says so. Under fan-out this gets worse, not better (§5): one full suite measured 3.5× slower with several racing on one machine.
  - **A targeted selection must name FILES, not keyword filters.** A `-k`-style filter still collects the whole suite before filtering, so a "quick" gate costs the full collection anyway.
  - **Any number you quote — a test count, a timing, a baseline — is measured by you, in the tree the dev will actually work in.** A count copied out of a README, a sibling branch, a previous brief, or the post-merge trunk is a claim with a shelf life: a branch legitimately behind the trunk has a lower count, and an absolute figure turns that into a false failure signal. And **one run is not a baseline in a repo with a known flake** — run it twice, or grep the repo's docs for the flake and name it.
- **For a test that guards a wiring fix, require RED-BEFORE-GREEN evidence in the `done_when` itself.** A criterion that only requires green cannot detect a vacuous guard: one round shipped 18 green tests that all passed with the fix reverted, and only the cross-family reviewer caught it. Ask for the failing output against the unfixed code, pasted.
- **For any change to product source, `done_when` MUST include the project's full unit suite**, not just the targeted test or E2E. A narrow acceptance command hides regressions in the code the change didn't target — a fix can pass its own new E2E while breaking an existing unit test, and a green narrow gate reads as "done". The targeted test proves the feature; the full suite proves you didn't break the rest. **Where the full suite exceeds the harness cap, this moves to you** (see above): the dev gets the targeted modules plus a collection check, and CI plus your own single local run is the full-suite gate — which is where iron rule #1 put it anyway.
- **For any layout/visual task, the first dispatch carries an explicit visual spec** — sizes, positioning, per-breakpoint order, spacing, transitions. E2E cannot see layout, so a behavior-only contract yields wrong positioning that no test catches and only visual QA finds, wasting a redispatch. Vague directions ("alternate the sides", "make it balanced") must be spelled out per case, as a *measured* criterion rather than a description — "fixed, content-appropriate width" is exactly the vagueness this rule forbids, and it shipped a clipped field at two of four breakpoints. Name the viewports, and name what must not clip.
- **Settle the visual VERIFICATION PATH in the plan, before dispatching — not at the gate.** Name the fixture that makes the change visible and who builds it. One UI change reached an open PR with the mandatory visual QA structurally unreachable: seeing it required a seeded reference dataset that existed in no environment, the dev may not start a server, and no seed fixture shipped it. If the fixture does not exist, **building it is its own task**, not an afterthought — otherwise the visual gate gets declared open instead of met. Equally: **make the two-theme requirement conditional on the app actually having a theme mechanism** — grep for one before writing the bar into a brief, or the dev spends its turns proving a negative.
- **For any feature request, ask: after this lands, can a PERSON do the thing?** One dispatch specified where a value lives, who may set it and what it changes, and never said who *uploads* it — the branch shipped four API routes and zero frontend, so the feature was reachable only by `curl` and the human caught it, not the orchestrator. If the answer needs a second dispatch, say so in the plan.
- **Partition parallel work by FILE and by SYMBOL, never by feature area.** Feature-area partitioning reads as disjoint and isn't: three slices of one epic each needed a dependency provider for the same new client, so all three wrote into the same region of the same file, and two of them independently created the same new shared module (an add/add conflict of an entire file). Enumerate the shared plumbing every slice will touch — dependency providers, route registration, settings, audit enums, example env files, and any new shared symbol the consumers would each otherwise create — and assign each to **exactly one** slice, ideally the prerequisite one, so the consumers only import it. Doing this up front turned a three-file add/add conflict into one additive enum in the next wave.
- **When a dispatch adds a timeout, an abort signal or any other BOUND to a call, require the dev to enumerate what the caller does with the new failure mode.** Bounding a call changes *which failure paths are reachable*, not just how long it waits, and every round of one four-round run created a new indeterminate state that the code then treated as determined — culminating in an indeterminate payment lookup acknowledged to the provider as HTTP 200, with no record and no retry. Two corollaries worth stating in the prompt: a fetch is bounded only if the abort stays armed **through the body read**, not just the response headers; and a client that resolves an aborted query as an empty result rather than throwing turns your bound into silent data loss.
- **When a fix DISSOLVES a finding rather than patching it, require the failure modes of the NEW mechanism to be enumerated too.** One fix moved from a cache to render-time derivation, correctly closing the staleness window — and opened a retained-stale-data window on the refetch-error path, which the code comment then asserted away as impossible. A new mechanism is a new set of failure modes, and the dev checked only the old set.
- **Assemble every prompt from `DEV_BRIEF.md` + the task. Never from a previous prompt.** A fix-round prompt built by copying a sibling task's header carried that sibling's instruction to create a specific test file — which is a 587-line file on the other branch, so obeying it would have produced a head-on collision at rebase. A stale file path in a copied header is indistinguishable from an instruction.
- **Ship symbol names, not line numbers.** A line number is a fact with a shelf life measured in merges: two corrected line numbers in one brief were stale again by the time the dev read them. Name the symbol and let the dev grep. Same rule for a base commit: say "rebase onto `origin/<trunk>` as it stands when you start", never a SHA — a base SHA in a brief goes stale within the hour on a busy repo.
- **Qualify an issue reference as `owner/repo#N` in any cross-repo dispatch.** A bare `#N` resolves against the worktree's repo, which is the wrong one whenever the work and the ticket live apart — a dev burned effort establishing that the referent did not exist, and could not write its findings to the issue as instructed.
- **Pre-verify the build toolchain IN THE DIRECTORY THE DEV WILL USE — not in your warm checkout.** Establish a green baseline yourself first, so a dev's failure is attributable to their change rather than to a broken environment. "Verify the repo builds" is not the rule; **"verify the dev's own `done_when` runs in the dev's own directory, dependency install included"** is. A fresh worktree or clone has *no toolchain at all*, and that is the common case when you create N of them for a fan-out:
  - A baseline measured in a warm checkout, dispatched into a dependency-less clone, cost one dev its first attempt to a mid-build timeout and its retry to a half-written dependency directory the kill had left behind: two attempts consumed on environment, zero on the task.
  - Four devs dispatched into fresh worktrees each paid the full setup themselves — four identical installs racing one disk, load average in the twenties, and two of them showing zero files written for thirty minutes while looking stalled. **Treat 20+ minutes of no writes in a fresh worktree as toolchain setup, not as a stall.**
  - Seed the environment as part of creating the worktree, in one step, not two: real dependencies (a copy-on-write clone from a sibling after checking the lockfile matches — **never a symlink**, which some bundlers reject outright), the browser bundles a full E2E run needs, and **the `.env` / test-env files, which `git worktree add` does not carry over** — one worktree missing them fell back to baked defaults pointing at a decommissioned host, and two smoke tests failed with page-load timeouts that read exactly like a code regression.
  - **A gate run against a dependency-less tree is VOID, never a fail.** A `command not found` exit, or a bare compiler exit 1 with no diagnostics, is a tooling signal — read as a verdict it would have condemned a good fix.
- **Deployment/topology contract — for any task touching deploy, config, hosts, or environments: inline the relevant `DEPLOYMENT.md` rows verbatim** (target environment, host/container, datastore, deploy branch + trigger, rollback) and require the dev to **update `DEPLOYMENT.md` in the same commit** if the change alters any of it. A non-native dev has no idea where the thing runs and won't go looking; without those rows inlined it will assume `main` deploys to "prod" and be wrong (validation playbook §6).
- **Explicit model AND effort — never inherit.** State both. Subagents often inherit the session's model and effort; if your session runs at a high tier, an inherited "light" task silently runs at the expensive tier — the most expensive way to do the cheapest work. Pin every dispatch (light→low, standard→medium, frontier→high). Any subagent the dev itself spawns must be pinned too.
- **Provider-vocabulary capture — for any task that sends a third party a string in THEIR vocabulary** (metric/scope/field/edge names, enum values, option keys, API version pins, header names): **you fetch the provider's doc page first, save it under `docs/research/` with its URL and fetch date, and quote the relevant extract verbatim into the prompt.** The dev implements exactly that extract; it must never be left to research or recall the vocabulary itself, because an unclear provider page turns into a guess. The dev-facing half of the rule is already in `DEV_BRIEF.md`. What is **yours** is the capture, the verbatim extract in the prompt, and pre-merge item 3b, where you probe each name and read the output with your own eyes (validation playbook §1c).
- **TDD, the development standards, and the honest-report contract are in `DEV_BRIEF.md`** — pasting it is what puts them in the prompt. Your job is not to restate them but to **reject work that violates them at pre-merge**: a green diff carrying speculative abstraction, a re-implemented helper, an untestable UI, or a silent failure path is a redispatch, and "the tests pass" does not excuse it.

## Development standards — they live in `DEV_BRIEF.md`

The engineering standards every dev must follow (YAGNI/DRY/KISS and least surprise, testability as a design constraint, E2E affordances built with the feature, and auditable logging on every decision and outcome) are **not restated here**. They are dev-facing prose, so they live in `DEV_BRIEF.md` as one versioned artifact and reach the dev by being pasted. A second copy inline would give two sources of truth for the same rules, and the one nobody edits is the one that gets read.

**What stays yours is the enforcement.** These standards go into your pre-merge review, not just into the prompt, and "passing tests" never excuses a violation:

- **YAGNI** — a green diff that adds a config knob, a hook, or an abstraction layer with no present caller is a redispatch, not a nice-to-have.
- **DRY** — a re-implemented helper that already existed is a redispatch; grep before you accept, the same way the dev was told to grep before writing.
- **KISS / least surprise** — if you need the dev to explain how it works, it is too clever for the next reader, who will be a different model.
- **Testability** — an un-observable failure path or a seam that cannot be stubbed is a design defect to send back, not a testing inconvenience to wave through.
- **E2E affordances** — a UI or flow change without stable test ids and awaitable states is a redispatch, exactly like a missing test. This is also what makes the run-close *Standing invariant* possible at all: the durable E2E guard can only exist because the affordances shipped with the first commit.
- **Auditable logging** — checked in detail at pre-merge item 5. A silent path, a swallowed error, or a business action with no `info` line is a redispatch (validation playbook §4).
- **Visual bar** — frontend-facing work gets screenshotted and inspected at pre-merge item 7. A visual defect is a redispatch; green tests never certify how something looks.

## Developer routing

**Family heuristic.** When you have more than one model family available, pick the one that fits:
- Prefer the family that best matches the repo's conventions and the task's shape; prefer a family that reads your project's convention files.
- For **independent second-opinion / verification passes**, deliberately use a *different* family than wrote the code — different lineages have different blind spots.
- Present-day examples of families are Claude, GPT/Codex, Grok, Gemini, Kimi, and local models — you may have any subset, each dispatched through its own CLI/tool. The methodology doesn't require a specific one; two different lineages are enough for the cross-family review gate.

**Model tier — cheapest that is good enough.** After picking the family, pick the tier by task demands, not habit:

| Tier | Use for |
|---|---|
| Frontier | architecture-shaping changes, gnarly concurrency/debugging, security-sensitive code, tasks a lower tier failed |
| Standard (default) | normal feature/bugfix implementation with TDD |
| Light | mechanical bounded work: renames, boilerplate, config plumbing, straightforward test additions, doc updates |

- Default to Standard. Downgrade to Light only when the task is mechanical AND bounded AND cheap to verify. Escalate to Frontier only with a concrete reason — "important project" is not one.
- **Effort before model.** Raising a model's reasoning effort is often a better lever than swapping the model. On failure, try higher effort before switching tier.
- Verification effort does NOT scale down with tier: a Light dev's report gets the same iron-rule verification as a Frontier dev's.
- **Let your own data demote a model, not your impression of it.** Log outcome per model (see Retrospective logging) and check the fail rates each retro. A model that reads as fine can carry a fail rate several times another's at the same tier — when the log says so, drop it out of default routing and escalate off it on the FIRST failure rather than the second. Re-check as more data lands; a demotion is a reading of the log, not a permanent verdict.

**Escalate on failure:** a task that fails your verification twice at one tier gets redispatched one tier up, with the failure findings included.

**Hard abort cap — a loop with no abort is a bill.** A single task gets at most **3 total dispatch attempts** across all redispatches and escalations combined. On the 3rd failure, STOP: log the task as abandoned, write what blocks it into a handoff, queue it for the human, move on. Never a 4th attempt — a task that beats three honest tries with findings fed back is a spec/design problem a bigger model won't fix. Not overridable by "it's almost working".

**Two exceptions, both narrow, both learned by hitting the cap wrongly:**
- **An INFRA death does not spend an attempt.** The cap exists for tasks that beat honest tries; a dispatch killed by a provider error, an organisation-membership failure, an overloaded upstream or a dropped connection carries **no task signal at all** and must not consume the budget. But log it, because **two consecutive infra deaths on one family is the signal to change family**, not to try that family a third time.
- **A converging task needs a convergence exception, not a flat count.** One run hit the cap with a single small, fully specified blocker left, and had to escalate to the human for want of a rule — while each round had demonstrably fixed a real defect the previous round had not seen. Where every attempt is closing findings rather than re-failing, say so explicitly and continue; where the same failure recurs, the cap stands.

**And a killed dev is not automatically a lost attempt.** Check the branch and the worktree before redispatching: more than once the commits were already in and verified, and a duplicate implementation was one command away.

## Cross-family review — the gate that pays

**For any Frontier-tier task or architecture-shaping / security-sensitive change, the reviewer MUST be a **different model family than the writer**, running in its own native tool. Relay the reviewer's verdict **verbatim** — never soften a FAIL into a summary. A FAIL routes back to a developer (counts toward the abort cap); you don't overrule it inline. Standard/Light tasks get the normal review; this is the extra gate for the changes that hurt most when wrong.

**This is the gate that pays, and the evidence is not close.** Repeatedly, independent families returned FAIL on builds where the full suite, lint, the build, a real-database run *and* the orchestrator's own visual QA were all green — and converged on the same defects. The worst of them were exactly the ones no test could see: a deleted user retaining full access to every customer conversation for hours (so the feature the human asked for did not work while looking like it did); an app-only API token for a whole tenant followed to an attacker-supplied host over plain HTTP; a background sweep closing live sessions because one statement's snapshot cannot see a concurrent commit. Run it for every send path, every auth path, and every scheduled mutation.

How to run it so it pays:

- **Route the review to a family that wrote NONE of the code.** Where that is impossible, scope the reviewer explicitly away from its own work.
- **A reviewer refusing, or appearing to die, is not a reason to skip the gate.** One run dispatched four families to obtain two usable verdicts.
- **Put mutation testing in the review brief explicitly** — "remove each safeguard, one at a time, and show which test died." Asked for, it found three real coverage gaps a green suite could never show; unasked, a reviewer will not think of it.
- **Ask explicitly for reasoning about isolation level and snapshot timing** on any background sweep or scheduled mutation. A green full suite says nothing about a concurrency window, and neither does a careful line-by-line diff read — that class was found by the reviewer both times and by the orchestrator neither time.
- **State your deliberate incompleteness up front.** A reviewer spent one of three blockers on a missing migration that was the orchestrator's own instruction — noise the orchestrator caused by not saying so.
- **Triage every finding against HEAD, not against the commit the reviewer read.** Reviewers read the tree they were given, and several findings are routinely already fixed; sending devs to redo finished work is how the gate loses credibility.
- **When the fixes are written by a party that raised some of the findings, get an independent re-review OF THE FIXES.**
- **A reviewer is a CLAIM GENERATOR, exactly like a dev.** Relaying a FAIL verbatim is not the same as believing every finding: of eight confidently cited defects in one review, three did not survive verification — and relaying all eight would have sent a dev chasing a phase that did not exist and a normalisation bug in code that already normalises. **Record the rejected findings somewhere durable**, or the next session re-investigates them.
- **But measuring the WRONG LAYER is worse than not measuring, because it produces a number that feels like evidence.** One review claimed a budget did not bound a phase, arguing from a generic per-query constant. The orchestrator measured the raw datastore round-trip, found it a hundredfold cheaper, declared the finding overstated, filed the rejection and merged. The first production run consumed the entire budget in that phase and processed one item per cycle — the bug the whole run existed to fix was still open after deploying the fix. Three rules out of it: **measure the layer the claim is about** (per-item *application* cost, not a proxy one level below it); **a finding whose conclusion is cheap to guard against gets guarded even when its arithmetic looks wrong**; and **prefer the reviewer's direction over your own refutation whenever that refutation rests on a proxy measurement you chose yourself.**
- **Every low-confidence or "plausible" finding gets one of three explicit dispositions before merge: verified real, verified not-real with the evidence, or cross-referenced to an existing tracked issue. Silence is not a disposition.** Handling was inconsistent inside a single batch — one plausible finding was investigated and proved real, another was skipped and was also real, already filed the day before, and later reproduced against production. The cheapest first step is grepping the open issues for the same component.

## Dispatch mechanics — deaths, reports and sandboxes

**Dispatching non-native subagents.** A subagent in a different tool gets no session context: the prompt must be self-contained — absolute repo paths, the task verbatim, relevant conventions inlined, plus the same TDD + honest-report contract. Verification is unchanged: their report is a claim like any other. Two constraints that decide the shape of the task:

- **A one-shot CLI mode (`-p`/`--print`) has a bounded turn budget — don't hand it a long multi-step loop.** Write + build + serve + run E2E + commit in a single one-shot call exhausts the turns mid-task and returns partial work (or nothing) while reading like it ran. Decompose instead: the dev WRITES the files (bounded, one deliverable), the orchestrator RUNS and verifies. Interactive/resumable modes are for the iterative work.
- **Know what each sandbox cannot do — and make it a ROUTING decision before dispatch, not a discovery after.** The cheapest form of this check: walk the `done_when` line by line and ask of each line whether it can run **with no network and no datastore**. On one task that would have caught the block on the very first line, instead of after the dispatch.
  - Some sandboxes have **no network at all** — not merely no browser: no loopback (so no local database, no dev server, no `localhost` anything), no remote (so no fetch and no push), and no writable user-level package cache (so the repo's own task-runner targets die before the first line of work). Anything needing a real database, a browser, a dev server or a push belongs on a family that can reach them. Where the work itself still suits that family, **decompose: the sandboxed dev writes, the orchestrator runs the networked half — and say so in the prompt**, or it burns turns discovering the wall. One such dispatch delivered a whole feature in six commits with 150 tests skipped, 36 fixture errors and a failed push; the orchestrator ran the database half and found two real defects.
  - **Isolation choice is a function of the dev FAMILY, not a default.** A local agent → a linked worktree is fine. A sandboxed agent → **a clone, always** (see §5), *and* the clone must sit in a path the sandbox may write: a session scratch directory under the system temp tree often is not one, and a git operation there dies before touching anything. Three sandboxed devs were once dispatched into linked worktrees past a rule that states this explicitly; all three edited files and then could not commit.
  - Even in a clone, a sandbox may still refuse the index lock. The working pattern is to tell the dev up front to **try once and stop**, and have the orchestrator commit.
  - **A foreground dispatch through any wrapper with its own hard timeout is structurally unreliable for anything non-trivial.** It killed two separate pieces of work in one day. Anything beyond a bounded, minutes-long edit goes detached and gets polled.
- **A "role-larp" report has a cheap detector: it speaks in the ORCHESTRATOR's vocabulary.** The failure — a dev that reads a global convention file mandating orchestration, declares itself the orchestrator, reports a routing table and an armed monitor, and writes zero code — recurred five times across families even with `DEV_BRIEF.md` pasted verbatim, because the brief's role override sits kilobytes above the task and loses to the convention file the dev reads *afterwards*. Position beats emphasis. Three fixes that measurably held: put the anti-larp guard **first in the prompt, ahead of the brief**; phrase it as a prohibition on **specific observable commands** rather than as a role description; and **make the first deliverable a commit** ("first tool call is `git status`, second writes the failing test, third commits it"). It is nondeterministic — a sibling dev on the same prompt shape did not larp — so detect as well as prevent: **grep the report for dispatch/tier/account/monitor vocabulary before reading it as work**, because a real dev report never contains it.

**Never truncate a dispatch report.** Do not pipe a dev or background command through `tail`/`head`/`sed` — write the FULL stdout to a file and read it whole. A truncated report is an unverifiable claim: you'd be "verifying" against a mutilated report, which breaks iron rule #1. If the report is genuinely too large for your context, hand the whole file to a read-only subagent to summarize — never lose it to a pipe.

**And the writing side of that same rule: commits survive a death, reports do not.** This is the asymmetry that decides how you shape a dispatch.

- **For any dispatch whose VALUE IS the long final message — a review, an audit, an investigation, anything that produces no code — require the report be WRITTEN TO A FILE, with stdout carrying only a one-line summary.** A captured stdout can lose the *head* of a long message with nothing piped anywhere: one review came back beginning mid-sentence inside finding #7, findings 1–6 gone, and finding #1 turned out to be the major one. **A report missing its most severe findings is worse than no report, because it reads complete.**
- **A review has a worse risk ratio than an implementation.** A dev killed at minute thirteen leaves commits; a reviewer killed at minute thirteen leaves nothing. So the file-first safety net matters double for reviews, and splitting a large review into two smaller batches beats risking total loss.
- **Read-only mode and an incremental-write safety net are incompatible — do not order both.** One review prompt demanded read-only *and* progressive findings written to a scratch file; the sandbox blocked all writes including the safety net, and thirteen minutes of work left nothing but a tool-call trace. Grant write access to that one path explicitly, forbid repo edits in the prose, and check `git status` afterwards.
- **Require counter-proposals to be recorded AS THEY ARE FOUND**, in a file or a commit message — not saved for the final report. A dev flagged a false premise of the orchestrator's mid-run and that finding died with the report on a timeout; the code survived, the correction did not.
- **Every dispatch gets a UNIQUE output path.** A redispatch that reuses the previous run's path truncates the evidence you need to judge the previous run — and an empty log then reads as death rather than as clobbering. That misreading led straight to a second dev launched into a live worktree.
- **An empty report is not evidence of a failed dispatch — and should still be treated as one, by policy.** Three consecutive dispatches once exited zero with not one sentence of report; the work was good in all three cases, but every claim had to be reconstructed from the diff and re-run from scratch, which silently doubles the orchestrator's cost. Re-dispatch asking *only* for the report against the commits that already exist, rather than paying to verify blind. (And a run of them across accounts is a mechanical fault to investigate, not a behavioural one.)
- **A dev report containing no file paths and no commit SHAs did not write code**, however good the analysis reads. Check the worktree before reading the prose.

**Background dispatch is where silent failures live.** A background job that dies can leave no notification at all, so you find out at your next status tick instead of when it happened — long after the wall-clock was wasted.
- **A readiness check often proves less than it claims.** "Authenticated: yes" may only test that a credential file EXISTS, not that the token inside is still valid — an expired token then kills the job instantly with "not signed in". **Foreground-probe the family once per session** with a tiny task before trusting it; setup passing is necessary, not sufficient.
- **Prefer foreground with a generous timeout** for anything correctness-critical or multi-step. Reserve background for genuinely long, low-stakes work.
- **If you must background:** poll the log within ~30 s for auth/startup errors before believing it started, re-poll every status tick, and require a **mid-run commit checkpoint** in the prompt so a dead run still leaves partial work on the branch.
- On a first-dispatch auth death, re-login is the human's step — meanwhile auto-route the task to another family rather than burning the run waiting.
- **A long dispatch must NOT be a harness-tracked background job.** It inherits that job's lifecycle and dies with it — two consecutive dispatches were killed mid-task that way, one at 25 minutes and one at 3, the second having produced only its startup banner. Launch long work **detached** and watch it by its recorded id; reserve harness-tracked background for short-lived polling. Corollary: never launch a dispatch from inside a wrapper whose own lifetime is shorter than the dev's.
- **The harness's own task-notification exit code may describe the WRAPPER, not the dev.** Read the dispatch's own recorded return code out of its output file; a wrapper's zero exit says nothing about whether the agent finished.
- **Check that the dev's permission mode permits the STEPS the brief requires, not just the file edits.** A mode that auto-approves edits but not shell commands leaves `git add`/`git commit` waiting for an approval nobody gives in a non-interactive run: the dispatch returned zero, the report said PASS, and the clone held a modified file with zero commits. The fix is a **narrow explicit allowlist** — deliberately excluding push, remote CLIs, delete, privilege escalation and network fetch, because pushing is the orchestrator's decision after verification — and never a blanket permission bypass, which a safety classifier caught the one time it was tried globally.
- **A recurring monitor that is ALLOWED to dispatch must check for in-flight work as its FIRST action**, not as part of classifying state. A dispatch-capable monitor plus any other retry path (a manual retry, a rescue loop) is a concurrency hazard by construction — two dispatch paths and no mutual exclusion is a race, however careful each one is alone.

**Orchestrator fallback — plan for your own family dying.** If the orchestrator's own model/account becomes unavailable mid-run (limits exhausted, outage), orchestration moves to another family: while your session still responds, write `HANDOFF.md` and carry the context over, then keep orchestrating there. The workflow contract has to travel IN the handoff — the other tool does not read this skill or your repo convention files, so `HANDOFF.md` must restate the iron rules, point at the pre-merge checklist, and carry current task state. A fallback you thought about after the session died is a fallback you don't have.

**Secrets & data governance — HARD BLOCK for hosted third-party families.** A dispatch prompt is data you are handing to an external provider. Never put credentials of any kind — API keys, passwords, tokens, SAS/connection strings, private keys, `.env` contents — into a prompt sent to a hosted third-party dev family. Reference secrets by name/placeholder and let the code read them from the environment at runtime; if a task genuinely cannot be expressed without a live secret, keep it on a family you trust for that data (or do it yourself).
- **Kimi (Moonshot): HARD BLOCK — no exceptions.** Never share any API key, password, or other credential with a Kimi subagent, in the prompt or in any file you hand it. If a task would require exposing a secret to Kimi, route it to a different family instead. This is not overridable by convenience or deadline.
- Apply the same caution, at your own data-governance threshold, to any other hosted family; local models you run yourself are the exception.
- This mirrors the logging rule below: secrets never reach a log, and they never reach an external dev prompt either.
- **The rule binds YOU too, and the way it breaks is redaction you wrote blind.** Verifying a hardcoded-credential finding, an orchestrator wrote a pattern-based redaction over file contents it had not read; the pattern did not match the real line shape, so a fragment of a live credential was echoed into the session transcript — and the hash comparison in the same command was fed the same garbage, so its verdict was unreliable *and* it had already leaked. **Never redact a secret by pattern-matching over content you have not seen. To compare two secrets, extract and compare them inside one process and print ONLY a boolean** — never a transformed copy of the source.
- **Before any suite authenticates against a base URL, assert the base URL's IDENTITY.** A wrong target turns a test run into a credential disclosure: an override exported *before* sourcing a dotenv file was silently overridden back by that file, and the port it named was held not by a stale local server but by a tunnel to an unrelated remote host — so the setup step submitted the test account's real email and password to a stranger's login form. Two mechanics: **source the dotenv file BEFORE any override, or better, pass the override inline on the command** so nothing can clobber it; and assert the expected application identity at that origin **before any credential is typed**. Checking that the port was free is not checking what answers on it.

## Red flags — STOP, you are rationalizing

- "It's a one-line fix, faster to do it myself" → the classic failure. Redispatch.
- "The report is detailed / the dev has been reliable" → detail and history are not evidence. Verify this one.
- "Release train leaves in 20 minutes" → your unreviewed code on the train is the bigger risk.
- "I verified my own fix, it's fine" → self-certification is not verification.
- "I'll verify all tasks at the end in bulk" → per-task verification; failures compound.

| Excuse | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Faster to fix it myself" | Your fix skips TDD and review — you just became an unreviewed developer. Redispatch is one call. |
| "The pasted test output proves it" | Output in a report is text, not a test run. Run it yourself. |
| "Senior dev, reliable all week" | The report in your hand can still be stale or fabricated. Verify. |
| "Deadline forces an exception" | The workflow exists precisely for high-pressure moments. No exceptions. |

# Validation playbook — what to actually check (hard-won)

Passing tests are necessary, not sufficient. These are the failure classes that slip through "all green" reports and cost real production breakage. Treat each as a mandatory check.

## 1. Verify against REALITY, never against your own construction
The single most expensive mistake: verifying a change against a copy YOU built to match your ASSUMPTION, instead of the real target. (A change "verified" against a throwaway DB built from a hand-written schema fixture — the fixture was wrong, so production 500'd on an invalid column the moment it ran.)

- When code runs against a production/external system (DB, API, blob store, queue), the authoritative verification hits the **real target**, or an exact snapshot **captured from** it — never a schema/mock you hand-wrote from docs or memory.
- Capture the contract FROM the live system (schema introspection, a real API response, a real file) and commit THAT as the fixture, with a header naming when/where it was captured.
- If you literally cannot reach the real target, do NOT substitute a self-made stand-in and call it verified. Say "unverified against real X" explicitly and hand off the exact command.
- Related trap: schema/behavior **drifts** between environments (dev vs staging vs prod, region A vs B). "It matches staging" ≠ "it matches prod". Verify the schema of the system the deployed process ACTUALLY targets.

## 1b. "It doesn't exist yet" is the most dangerous conclusion — verify prior art before greenfield
A subagent investigated and reported that a foundational subsystem "does not exist", so a whole parallel implementation got built from scratch — while a COMPLETE version already sat on the main branch, merged a week earlier. The result: two different implementations, colliding migration numbers that could not be merged, and an entire run wasted. A negative that authorizes building from scratch has the highest blast radius of any claim, so verify it hardest.

- Before building any net-new subsystem/foundation: `git fetch`, then scan the log across ALL branches, the branch list, and the PR list (open AND recently merged), and grep the code for the tables/routes/migrations you're about to create. Confirm the thing truly isn't already on the main branch, a sibling branch, or an in-flight PR.
- The investigation must run against real git history + remote + PRs, **not a single working-tree snapshot** — a checkout can lag the main branch by many commits and PRs. That's exactly how it happens: the start-of-run explore reads a stale base and concludes "nothing exists".
- Before minting a migration / schema-version number, confirm that number isn't already taken on another branch. Two parallel `0011`s that differ are instantly unmergeable.
- **Filing an ISSUE is the same class of act, and it is the one everybody exempts.** Opening an issue asserts *this is not already tracked* — one was filed with a repro and acceptance criteria for something an existing issue had described three weeks earlier, and described better: it also covered two adjacent paths **and already carried a pending product question the human answered later the same day**, which was nearly lost. One search of the existing issues before every `create`, every time. It is cheap, and here it would also have surfaced three related instances — which is what made the eventual umbrella issue worth having.
- When the human's ask is "simplify / fix the existing X", the default assumption is that X EXISTS — go find it before proposing to build one. If your reading of the request has you building rather than changing, re-read it: you probably mislocated the existing thing.

## 1c. Provider vocabulary: documentation is a HYPOTHESIS, a live call is the evidence — and "the dev says it verified" is neither

Every string you send a third party in *their* vocabulary — metric names, scope names, field/edge names, enum values, option keys, API version pins, header names — is a claim about their system that only their system can settle.

The incident: four analytics metric names shipped under the source comment *"all four confirmed present in vN"*, citing a research document by file and section. That document was sourced from the provider's own reference and changelog, and the deployment did run vN. Live vN rejected two of the four, the provider rejects the **entire** call when one name is bad, and the endpoint therefore returned nothing for every record — for as long as it had existed. It was found weeks later, by accident, during an unrelated probe. Two failures, and the second one is the orchestrator's: **the documentation was read, cited precisely, and was still wrong**, so "I checked the docs" is not a verification result; and **a claim laundered into a source comment is still a claim**, more dangerous there than in a report because the next reader treats it as settled history.

- **A provider-vocabulary constant may not merge until the orchestrator has seen it accepted by the live provider.** Not the dev — you. One command whose output you read.
- The docs stay mandatory as the *starting point*: the dev records the URL and the date fetched, not a remembered name, and not a citation to another document inside the repo (that is how a stale fact gets a second life). At the incident above, the documentation's stated replacement names turned out correct and live-accepted, while two names reasoned out from a provider blog post were both rejected — the docs were the only reliable source of candidate names, and the live call the only reliable judge of them.
- **A comment may not assert "confirmed"/"verified" without saying by what.** `# confirmed present in vN (docs §9)` is a citation; `# probed live 2026-01-14: accepted, 1 row` is evidence. Require the second form, with the date — a live fact has a shelf life, and the date is what lets the next reader judge it.
- **Probe each name individually, never only as a set.** A provider that rejects the whole request on one bad name tells you nothing about which one; per-name probing turns a useless generic error into an actionable table.
- **Design for one name dying.** A fixed vocabulary set is a total outage the day the provider deprecates one member. Degrade: drop the rejected name, keep the rest, and log loudly *which* name was dropped — a silently shrinking result set is its own defect.
- **The four-step capture procedure, in order, none of them skippable:** (1) you fetch the provider's doc page yourself and save it under `docs/research/<provider>-<topic>.md` with its URL and fetch date; (2) the dispatch quotes the relevant extract verbatim, so the dev implements exactly that rather than researching it; (3) re-fetch and diff before relying on a capture older than the change, updating the date — the repo in the incident *had* a local capture, cited by file and section, and it was months stale, because a local document nobody re-fetches is a fossil with a citation; (4) probe live anyway.
- **When a doc mentions a deprecation, capture its DATE and SCOPE, not just the version number.** Provider deprecations are frequently date-based across *all* API versions, which makes a version pin worthless — while a "deprecated above vN" note reads misleadingly like "fine on vN".
- **Cheapest way to run the probe: inside the deployed environment**, where the real credential already lives, so nothing is copied out. Iterate the constant one member at a time and print only name, status, verdict. Commit that as a script, so the next version bump re-runs it instead of re-reasoning about it.
- **The evidence goes INTO THE ISSUE as acceptance criteria:** the documentation with URL, fetch date and the load-bearing sentences quoted verbatim; the live probe table with one request per candidate name, its verdict, the credential/target used and the date, **including the rejected rows**; and `Done when` written as checkable statements over those two tables. Without it, the next dev goes back to the same unclear provider page that produced the bug, and the rejected rows are what stop someone re-proposing a name that does not work.
- **Version bumps are the recurring trigger.** Pinning a provider to a new API version silently re-opens every vocabulary question that version answers differently. A version-pin PR that does not re-probe the vocabulary is incomplete, no matter how green its tests are.
- When a live probe is genuinely impossible (no credential, no test account), say **"unverified against live provider"** in the PR and in the code comment, and hand over the exact probe command. Never let silence imply it was checked.

## 1d. Every fact in YOUR OWN dispatch is a claim — and yours are the ones nobody checks

The largest single failure class in the log, and it is not about code. Over one month the most frequent recorded defect was **an orchestrator asserting something in a dispatch prompt that turned out false** — a line number, a call site, a baseline, a mechanism, a safety property, the existence of a file. Nearly every one was caught by the *dev*, and each cost that dev part of its attempt to disprove. A dev's report gets iron rule #1; **your prompt gets nothing, and it is read as authoritative.**

One sentence of counter-discipline: **say what you MEASURED and HOW — never what you assume, remember, extrapolate or transcribe.**

- **State the mechanism of measurement beside the fact**, so the dev knows which half is measurement and which is your summary ("there is one unconditional worker" — grepped the registrations, never opened the classes; there were two). A count belongs in the prompt as *the command and its output*, not as your number.
- **An unmeasured REASSURANCE is worse than an unmeasured risk: a risk gets checked, a reassurance gets believed.** "It already closes all open rows first, so it is safe under the new constraint" was false — a concurrent uncommitted row is invisible to that snapshot — and only survived because the dev was told to verify rather than assume. If you state a safety property, state how you established it.
- **Read the thing before describing it.** Read the migration before briefing how a table binds; the guard before asserting what it refuses; the constant before naming it as a field's source; the repo before citing its convention file or a tool as available. Each of those shipped a false premise, most of them one command from the truth.
- **A NEGATIVE authorises building (§1b) — and a POSITIVE authorises a conclusion.** "No deploy script, no CI deploy" sent a dev to build a 359-line duplicate of an existing deploy: the search had **failed silently** (a shell glob matching nothing is not an empty result) and the deploy lived in a *sibling* repo. So check your search actually ran, run it a second way, and cover sibling repos. Conversely, **naming a symbol as the blocker claims it is REACHED** — twice in one night a rule was blamed while an earlier return in the same function made it dead code. **Read a function from its opening brace and find every return above the line you care about; one return is not control flow.**
- **A cause recorded in a repo doc, an old PR body or an issue is a claim with a timestamp.** Quote it as a hypothesis to test. One documented root cause, repeated into a dispatch as established, measured **zero** occurrences over thirty runs; the real failure was an ordering race.
- **Pointing a dev at an existing implementation as "the pattern" is a claim about a DIFFERENT audience — say which of its properties are load-bearing.** Faithful copies inherited a local-vs-UTC calendar mismatch, an authorisation decision resting on a join that is one-to-many in the direction the check depends on, and an admin-convenience default nobody re-decided for a customer endpoint. Name the behaviour you want, not the code to imitate.
- **Before handing over a FORMULA, enumerate every signal the data model carries for that decision** — grep the type and every existing consumer. A formula reads as authoritative and narrows what the dev considers: one omitted a second flag two other pages already treated as authoritative, and a cross-family FAIL was what forced it out.
- **When you enumerate call sites, verify each exhibits the pattern** — an over-broad list makes the dev cause a regression *while obeying you*.
- **State the symptom and the evidence; let the dev find the mechanism.** A mechanism written into an issue from a log excerpt, then into a brief, becomes fact: right symptom, wrong cause, and a dev that trusted it would have built a wait around a real bug. Check a worked example reproduces, and do its arithmetic rather than transcribing it.
- **Derive the mechanism FROM the values in the artefact, not from the code you happened to read.** One diagnosis was refuted by two facts already on screen — a timestamp only one path can produce, and a value the alleged mechanism cannot produce at all. **A mechanism that cannot produce the observed string is not the mechanism.**
- **The human proposes the GOAL, not the implementation — do not adopt their phrasing as technical fact.** Twice in one night an acceptance criterion named a channel that structurally cannot deliver: a group chat that only broadcasts to human members, and a proximity-ranged channel used to pair two entities far apart, one offline. **An architectural assumption in a brief has §1b blast radius** — it stands the whole dispatch when wrong, and one grep at who a message is actually sent to settles it.
- **When two sets of names disagree, ask which is the INPUT and which the generated OUTPUT** before asking which is right. Drift is meaningful only between two sources of the same thing; between an input and its generated artefact a difference is expected. A dispatch premised on that confusion would have fixed nothing.
- **A generated artefact is not a complete one.** A generated API document handed to a third-party developer documented no errors, no security scheme and no response bodies, because that codebase returns its error catalogue through a helper rather than by raising. Audit before handing over.
- **A path is not provenance.** Three files handed over as "real third-party output" were one empty file and two synthetic fixtures written by a script in the same tree; a dev that trusted it would have reported the opposite of the truth. Say how you established a file is genuine — or say that you could not.
- **Say what you sampled, not what you extrapolated** ("zero of the eight I sampled", where the population was fifteen), and verify a pairing before quoting an identifier next to a name.
- **A finding that exists only in your handoff is a finding the dev does not have.** Put it in the prompt or accept it will not be fixed.
- **Offering two options is itself a trap** — a dev reads A/B as a closed list, and one of them may be impossible. "Here is the problem and my hypothesis, find the right solution" produced a third and better answer precisely because the dev had a mandate to contradict. Where you do offer options, say *measure both before choosing*.

## 1e. An acceptance criterion that would pass with a WRONG implementation

Four in a single day, all the orchestrator's, all caught by devs. Common denominator: **the criterion was written from how the code was imagined, not from looking.** Ask of every criterion *"would this still pass against a broken implementation?"* — and answer it by breaking the thing on purpose and watching the criterion fail.

- **A guarantee that lives in the SCHEMA must be asserted about the schema or the query plan, never about a query result.** A correct query returns the right answer over a broken schema too, just slower. Assert against the catalog (indexes, constraints, types, collation) or the plan — and in a plan the discriminating detail is *how* the index is used, not that its name appears.
- **Run every grep-based criterion against the current code first: it MUST find something.** One grepped for a literal that could never match, because the production string carried a comma inside the quotes — it would have passed a zero-line implementation.
- **A guard asserting PRESENCE rather than ATTRIBUTION is theatre.** A substring check proves co-occurrence; a neighbouring field satisfied it, so a wrongly-labelled value passed. Assert the value in its labelled position. Keep negative assertions narrow, too: a rule forbidding any company name anywhere false-positived on a legitimate third party and would have blocked the next correct edit — so it gets deleted, which is worse than never having had it.
- **A fixture that bypasses the real path cannot see the real defect** — a removal test writing straight into the cache never exercised the invalidate-and-refetch path where the bug lived.
- **A test pinning the CURRENT version of something is a landmine for every future change.** Assert the property (one head, this revision reachable), not the value.
- **Check the criterion is representable at all.** One described a database state the constraints make impossible; one required a third-party client that fails for an unrelated reason; one demanded a real credential in a repo whose fixtures deliberately scrub those variables, so the test was **dead by construction** while looking like coverage.
- **A performance criterion must name the input DISTRIBUTION**, or a dev will honestly measure the best case.
- **A green GATE is a claim exactly like a dev's report.** Running a gate and reading its exit code is not verifying what it *proves*: one passed 8/8 while proving only that a key was bound in a config, not that anything read it — so a dead key passed as valid. **Mutation-test the gate: find a case that should NOT pass, and try it.** Every time, not only when a gate looks suspicious. (Cheap companion: have a repo gate scan tracked files for leftover merge-conflict markers — one verification passed 6/6 on a tree containing them, because they are valid text in Markdown.)

## 1f. Your own probes, tools and instruments lie in WELL-FORMED ways

Four times in one run a probe returned a plausible, well-formed, **wrong** answer and was nearly acted on. The hardest class to notice, because nothing looks broken: **green-looking output from the wrong source.**

- **Never pipe a verification run through `head`/`tail`/`sed`.** The pipeline returns the *pager's* exit code, so a failed suite reads as exit 0; a truncated log still ends in a plausible tail; and what gets cut on a red run is the failure's identity. Redirect to a file, read the file, take the exit code with no pipe in between. (Shell trap: the array carrying a pipeline's real exit codes is named differently per shell and is silently empty in the wrong one.)
- **A windowed read cannot support a NEGATIVE claim.** A headline finding — "no feature test is executed by any gate" — rested on a match window that cut a workflow's file list mid-way, with seven feature specs just below the cut. A negative claim about coverage has §1b blast radius and deserves the same proof: dump the whole file or the whole matched block.
- **A liveness probe whose failure mode is indistinguishable from the condition it detects is worse than none** — it triggers a false rescue. Never match a command-line pattern across the whole machine (it matched another project's session; separately it returned zero while both devs were provably committing), and **never let a `|| default` fallback collapse probe-failure into the negative reading.** Monitor by a **recorded id that owns the work**.
- **A log that echoes the prompt contains every sentinel the prompt names.** An unanchored search for a verdict marker matched the orchestrator's own instruction inside a reviewer's log, and the human was told the review had FAILED before it produced any verdict. Anchor the pattern, skip past the prompt echo, or have the agent write its verdict to its own file.
- **When a probe reports something ALARMING or IMPLAUSIBLE, verify the probe before believing the alarm.** The base rate of a broken probe far exceeds the base rate of a dev deleting a test suite — which is what a diff-compacting wrapper made one diff look like. **Cross-check any probe once against a second source before trusting it for a whole run.**
- **A command-output-compressing proxy or wrapper in your shell is part of your evidence chain.** Measured in one month: merge commits silently stripped from a log listing (a merge read as linear, a branch looked lost), a count command reformatted into uselessness, a test summary truncated mid-output, and a **false-negative grep — zero matches for a string that is in the file.** That last one is the dangerous one, because grep-first completeness claims rest on it. Re-run anything load-bearing without the wrapper.
- **Verify what an instrument MEASURES before reporting it as a trend.** A container-stats CPU figure was reported all evening as instantaneous load; it averages over the container's whole lifetime, which looks reasonable and moves the right way with enormous lag — real load was a full core while the tool read 83–91 %. **The first sample belongs to verifying the instrument, not to the report.**
- **A metric that jumps after a change is not itself a regression — ask whether the UNIT changed.** A fourfold rise was the intended effect of replacing one shortcut with repeated honest attempts. Verify against the goal, not the proxy.
- **A tooling signal is not a verdict.** A missing-command exit, a bare non-zero with no diagnostics, a run against a tree with no dependencies, a skip reported through the same exit code as a failure (which nearly sent a healthy PR back) — all VOID. Always print the last line of output beside the exit code.
- **When your numbers disagree with a dev's report, suspect YOUR environment first and re-read the dev's caveats before anything else.** Twice in one run the dev had predicted the orchestrator's failure mode verbatim, in the very report being disputed.

**If you build your own tooling to make dispatch decisions, that tool is the highest-leverage instance of all of the above** — every dispatch trusts it silently, so a wrong answer there beats no tool. These came out of six rounds of critical findings on one such tool, and none is specific to what it decided:

- **A state it could not determine is NEVER the healthy state.** Fail safe toward *not acting*: unreadable, missing or malformed input classifies as unknown and takes the cautious branch. The most repeated finding was a value reaching "OK" without positive evidence.
- **Distinguish "wait a moment" from "exhausted", and "broken" from "unavailable".** Collapsing distinct conditions into one negative answer turned a recoverable state into a permanent one: an expired credential was indistinguishable from a rate limit, so it was never refreshed and never returned to rotation — a closed loop. Each condition gets its own exit code and its own printed remedy.
- **One constant answers exactly one question.** A single threshold serving both "is it expired?" and "is acting on it safe?" was the root cause of five consecutive rounds of critical findings. Split them, document which question each answers, and test that they stay split.
- **Name a field after the DECISION it drives; keep the raw diagnostic separate.** "Would one retry fix this?" is the caller's question; the raw underlying verdict is a weaker fact, and routing on it produces confidently wrong answers. Wire the human-readable line and the machine-readable payload to the *same* field, so a disagreement between them is a bug rather than a judgement call.
- **A tool that routes must ALWAYS print exactly one decision line — a missing answer is functionally identical to a wrong one.** Put a safety net at every such tool's entry point so a crash still yields its line (answer unknown, probe failed, here is the manual step) instead of a traceback, with the exception and a **scrubbed** message on the error stream. The net must never invent an optimistic answer and never hide the fault.
- **Validate the UNIT, not just the type.** Seconds and milliseconds are both numbers; accepting either yields a confident answer wrong by three orders of magnitude.
- **Never pass a secret on a command line** — give it to the child on its input stream, and assert in a test that it never appears in the arguments. When a diagnostic truncates a long value from the middle, make sure it cannot emit *half a token*.
- **Do not patch a residual with a GUESS about a process you do not control.** One guess about what a foreign client was doing to a shared credential destroyed that credential three times out of three before it was deleted. Where a residual is inherent to the setup rather than caused by your code, **document it and leave it unpatched**; a second guess is not a fix.
- **Fuzz the containers, not only the leaves.** An upstream schema change must end in a named schema error, never a traceback, and a well-formed response in an unrecognised shape must never read as an exhausted resource. Test the foreign-input path with mixed lists, absent keys, nulls and hung subprocesses, and assert that **an absent number never renders as zero.**

## 2. Integration seams between independently-green PRs
Two PRs each pass their own tests, git-merge cleanly, and the SEAM between them is dead. (One component wrote a value to store A; the other read it from store B → the reader always saw empty → the feature silently never ran in production, with every per-PR test green.)

- After merging PRs that share a surface (a settings field, a table, an adapter type, an auth token, an event contract), verify the **end-to-end integration**, not each side in isolation. Trace the actual data flow across the boundary.
- A clean `git merge` proves no textual conflict, NOT semantic compatibility.
- One source of truth per value. If PR A introduces an interim source and PR B introduces the real one, the merge must delete the interim.
- **Both sides being green is exactly what makes a seam invisible.** One side's test asserted the value is absent and was correct about its own side; the other's fixture always supplied it; no test on either side could see the contradiction, and it survived three dispatches and two orchestrator verification passes before a dev found it while working on something else. **Reading the OTHER side's tests belongs in any task that consumes a contract.**
- **A cross-cutting finding must become a dispatch or a tracked item in the same tick you find it — a note in the handoff is not a queue.** A handoff is per-run state you rewrite constantly, so anything in it that is not acted on in the same session evaporates quietly. That is exactly how the seam above was known, written down, and lost.
- **When a dispatch states a CARDINALITY rule, state how the collection is constructed and whether duplicates are possible.** "Apply this when there is one platform" invited a length check, but the list maps *accounts* to platform types and can therefore hold the same type twice — so the rule silently disengaged on the very network where it mattered, with a deduplicated collection sitting unused one line away. The defect lives in the gap between *one entry* and *one distinct value*.
- **Where a value is aggregated by a key, ask where that key comes from and whether it can differ in spelling.** One bucket key arrived as free text from two independent sources; nothing green can find that.
- **When two parallel tasks touch a shared contract and one changes it, send the coordination message IMMEDIATELY — do not wait for the report.** The other dev was building against attributes that were about to stop existing.
- **A semantic conflict caused by another branch REFACTORING the file goes back to a dev with the refactor named — never resolved by the orchestrator.** Taking the branch side would have re-created the duplication the refactor had just removed.

## 3. Test infra masks production tech
In-memory/SQLite/mocks are permissive; the production stack (a real RDBMS driver, real cloud services, a real webview) is not. Things that pass a mock and break on the real tech: a number written as a string, a type the driver can't bind, a whole set of columns that don't exist.

- A portable/mock test is necessary but **not sufficient** for code that runs against a specific production technology. Require at least one verification against the real tech, or flag the gap loudly and hand off the command.
- Enumerate the production-only failure surfaces per change: exact column set + types, NOT NULL constraints, driver type coercions (bool vs bit, uuid as string, tz-aware datetimes), auth header formats, chunked encoding, code signing, per-platform binaries.
- **A fixture for a contract must be DERIVED FROM the producing side's real payload.** Fixtures hand-written to satisfy the type declarations produce a suite that agrees with itself and with nothing else: three separate production defects in one session came from exactly that — blank names, an invented fallback, a blank page — with both sides green throughout. **A spec suite whose fixtures are typed from the same declarations it is meant to validate is evidence of nothing.**
- **When a defect class keeps shipping, suspect the INSPECTOR before the code.** Two defects survived a fully green suite because of how the suite looked, not what the code did: every assertion used a "contains" matcher, which structurally cannot see a doubled trailing character; and a document inspector did not tokenise the line-break operator, so every wrapped line was reported at the first line's baseline — the single position at which an overflow is invisible. An inspector that cannot express the defect will report clean forever.
- **Check what a test environment SCRUBS before writing an acceptance that depends on it.** A live round-trip test guarded by environment variables that the suite's own fixture deliberately deletes can never run: it looks like coverage and is dead by construction.
- **Test-infra masking has a routing twin: an override registered on the wrong application object.** Where a service mounts a sub-application, that sub-app carries its own dependency overrides — a fixture registered only on the parent leaves every child route silently reading the real (absent) dependency, and the tests pass as though the deployment had no such service at all.

## 4. Observability is a first-class deliverable — a failure you can't see remotely is a failed feature
When something breaks in production and it's invisible (the backend exception lived only in container stdout, the client shipped no failure event at all), support cannot reconstruct what happened.

- Everything important must be reconstructable from remote logs **without shell access**: backend errors persisted as queryable structured records, client failures shipped **immediately** (error-level flush), correlated across tiers by a single id per operation.
- A feature is NOT done if its FAILURE paths are silent. For every action ask: "when this fails next month, will support see why, remotely?"
- Never let a failure path silently swallow: no `except: pass`, no fire-and-forget without an error event, no state stuck in an intermediate value with no event emitted per retry/failure.
- Redaction is non-negotiable and orthogonal: passwords/tokens/keys never reach any log, on any tier.

## 5. Parallel agents: isolation is mandatory
Every rule here is a decision you make BEFORE dispatching, not a check afterwards.

- Every parallel agent gets its **own git worktree**. One issue → one agent → one worktree. Never two agents in one working tree.
- **A worktree is NOT isolation when the agents share any other exclusive resource — allocate every one of them, not just the checkout.** This was learned four separate ways:
  - **A datastore.** Four devs in four worktrees whose configs all default to the same host port: the first one up owns it and every other worktree silently connects to *its* database instead of failing loudly. That is cross-contamination, not a crash. Two suites truncating one database produced deadlocks and a hundred failures on a branch that was fine. **Assign a port and a database per parallel worktree before dispatching.**
  - **A restartable service.** Two devs on one machine, both entitled to restart the same service: one restarted it four times in seven minutes and destroyed the other's benchmark, which needs six minutes of undisturbed uptime. **A service restart is an exclusive resource exactly like a git index** — give the right to exactly one dev, or keep restarts yourself and let devs ask.
  - **The machine itself.** Contention is measurable, not theoretical: one full suite took 70 minutes with five racing and under 20 on a quiet machine; another took 47 minutes locally against ~11 in CI. And on a memory-bound host the OS kills co-resident agents *together*. **Budget the machine: cap heavy concurrency (~2), never hold a dev server while two agents run, queue full suites rather than fanning them out, and let CI be the primary full-suite gate.**
  - **Your own probes.** The isolation rule binds the orchestrator too. Ad-hoc probes run in a tree where a suite was running turned a 70-second run into 281 seconds with two failures that did not reproduce clean — and a verification suite pointed at the database a dev was using produced an 85-failure "baseline" that was entirely self-inflicted. A running test suite counts as someone using the checkout.
- **Do not relaunch a "stalled" background command without checking it is dead first.** A run producing no output is not a dead run — many test runners emit nothing until a whole file finishes, and one long suite was declared hung three times because the poll interval was shorter than the gap between its output lines. Check the command's *actual* runtime expectation and its output granularity before believing a hang, then check the process. Relaunching manufactures exactly the contention described above.
- **A sandboxed dev needs a CLONE, not a linked worktree, whenever the repo's `.git` sits outside its writable area.** A linked worktree keeps its index and the common git dir in the ORIGINAL location, so the dev can write files and then cannot commit them (`Operation not permitted` on the index lock — one dev implemented and green-tested half its task, then died there, correctly refusing to continue because its contract said commit before any long command). A clone's `.git` is self-contained inside the sandbox, preserving both isolation and commit capability; land the work afterwards by fetching the branch from the clone. "Its own worktree" is necessary, not sufficient — it must also be one the dev can commit in.
- **Never cancel an in-flight marker on a dead-pid basis unless the recorded pid OWNS the work.** PID liveness is a valid staleness test only when the process that wrote the marker is the process doing the job. A dispatcher that records its own pid and owns its subprocess qualifies; a thin launcher that records a short-lived pid while the task continues inside a shared long-running server process does not — two such records were read as leaked markers and one live job was cancelled, whose redispatch then put two devs in one checkout, the exact violation this section exists to prevent. **When in doubt treat the marker as LIVE and wait**: a wrongly cancelled live job is strictly worse than a stale record.
- **"Is this dev alive?" has exactly one honest answer shape: the process is gone AND its output is stale.** Every cheaper signal has produced a false death, and every false death ended with two writers in one tree — the violation this whole section exists to prevent. The catalogue, all real:
  - **Never declare a dev dead from log CONTENT.** A 186-byte log holding only an opening line of intent matched a known silent-death pattern perfectly; the process was alive, the file's mtime was seconds old, and it went on to produce a complete review. A known failure pattern raises suspicion; it does not substitute for the check.
  - **An unmoved HEAD is equally consistent with thinking hard and with never having started.** A dispatch that was *refused* (no capacity) wrote one line to its log and was counted as an in-flight dev for 55 minutes. **A dispatch is not launched until you have SEEN its log header** — read the first line within ~20 s of every launch and confirm it names the account and model rather than a refusal.
  - **A wrapper's pid is not the agent.** Killing the wrapper leaves the child running and committing; the shell reports success and you dispatch a second dev into a live worktree. Kill the child and **verify with `ps`** before anything else enters that tree. The same applies to a harness stop button: it kills the tracked shell, not the agent it launched.
  - **A forwarder and the task it forwarded are independent — the death of the forwarder says NOTHING about the task.** One task ran on, in a shared server process, for an hour after its forwarder was killed at a timeout; a `--resume` was issued against it and a second dev was sent into the same worktree. It was harmless only because the old task had stopped writing.
  - **A missing report is not a death, and a death produces no report.** A headless dispatch that dies emits nothing at all, so the monitor must treat *dispatch process gone* as the report — and go straight to running the `done_when` itself. Conversely, **a clean worktree plus a finished git operation is NOT a finished dispatch**: one merge went in at the moment a push landed, while the dispatch was still running its own gate for another 55 minutes. Wait for the completion notification and read the report before merging, or record the HEAD you verified and re-check it at merge time.
  - **Before verifying a branch built by a dispatch you did not launch, check for a live process in that worktree AND record HEAD — then re-check HEAD after the run.** A HEAD read once at the start is not a fact for the duration of a test run; a foreign dev moved it mid-suite.
  - **A tool refusing an operation is not proof of a blocked dev.** A workspace-trust warning only drops extra permission entries — defaults still permit work — and reading it as a permission block rerouted a task whose original dev was merely slow, and then had two agents in one tree. Prove a block with an actual denied-tool message in the log, never with an absence of output.
- The orchestrator NEVER runs builds, `git checkout`, or other state-changing ops in a checkout an agent is using. Use a separate worktree for your own builds/installs too.
- When an agent reports something odd about its environment ("files changing under me", "modified since read"), INVESTIGATE before dismissing — file-lock errors are real signals, not agent confusion.
- A test seen failing ONCE must be explained before merge. Distinguish env-flake (rerun isolates it) from code-flake (nondeterministic logic) — both block merge until understood.

## 6. Deployment & production-reality checks
Infrastructure, not application logic, tends to be the single largest failure class once a system is deployed — so treat topology and triage ORDER as first-class:

### `DEPLOYMENT.md` — the deploy + environment map is WRITTEN DOWN, in the repo (mandatory)
**The most repeated infra waste is re-deriving where the system actually runs and how it ships.** Every fresh session, every handoff, every non-native dev starts with zero knowledge of the topology — so it gets rediscovered slowly (grep, SSH, list the containers) or, worse, **assumed from memory and got wrong**: a deploy aimed at the wrong host, a "manual deploy" that was auto-on-push, a branch everyone believed was the prod trunk and wasn't. This is not a recall problem to fix with better memory. It is a **documentation deliverable, and producing it is part of the work.**

**Rule: every repo that deploys anywhere carries a `DEPLOYMENT.md` at its root, and keeping it true is part of every task that touches deploy, config, or topology.** NOT in `HANDOFF.md` (per-run, evaporates), NOT in your tool's memory (per-machine, per-model, invisible to a dev in another tool), NOT in a chat scrollback. In the repo, in git, where a dev of any family can read it.

**What it must answer, per environment (prod / staging / each region or tenant):**
- **Name + who uses it**, and which one is authoritative for real users.
- **Public entrypoint** — the URL/DNS that traffic actually hits, what terminates TLS, where that certificate lives and how it renews.
- **Where it physically runs** — host/instance/cloud resource, which container/service/unit serves it, which port, which reverse-proxy config file.
- **Which datastore that process really targets** — server, database name, and how it's wired (env var vs baked config — see config-precedence below). Spell out region/tenant splits; "the prod DB" is not an answer when there are two.
- **How it ships** — the deploy trunk **branch stated explicitly** (a repo whose prod branch is not `main` is exactly where this goes wrong), the trigger (push-auto-deploy / CI workflow / manual command), the literal command or workflow name, and who may run it.
- **How to roll back** — the actual command, not "revert the commit".
- **Where the logs are** and how to read them remotely, per tier (see §4).
- **Known gotchas** — what already bit here: a leaking container, an undersized database tier, a sibling host that looks like prod but isn't.
- **`last_verified: <date>` on every row.** A topology fact is a claim with a timestamp, exactly like a passing test (see *Standing invariant*). A row nobody re-checked in months is a lead, not a fact.
- **Secrets stay out.** Name the env var, the vault path, the credential's location — never the value. This file is committed, and in a public repo it is world-readable; write it so publishing it costs you nothing.

**When it gets written and updated:**
- **Whenever you map the topology** (next rule): the mapping's OUTPUT is a committed `DEPLOYMENT.md` diff. Discovering it and not writing it down means the next session pays the same cost again. If the file doesn't exist yet in a repo that deploys, **creating it is the first task of the run**.
- **Whenever reality contradicts the file:** fix the file in the SAME commit as the discovery, and update `last_verified`. A stale deploy doc is more dangerous than none, because it gets trusted.
- **Whenever a change alters how or where anything deploys** (new environment, new host, changed branch/trigger, moved datastore, new proxy rule): the PR that changes deployment updates `DEPLOYMENT.md`, or it is a **redispatch** — same as a missing test.
- **Point at it from the repo's convention file** so devs of every family find it, and **inline the relevant rows into any dispatch prompt** that touches deploy/config/infra — a non-native dev will not go looking.

**It is still a claim.** `DEPLOYMENT.md` removes *rediscovery*, not *verification*: before a prod deploy or during incident triage you confirm the live topology yourself (below). The difference is that you're checking a written hypothesis in seconds instead of rebuilding it from nothing, and each check either confirms the doc or corrects it.

- **Map the live topology yourself before any prod deploy OR prod incident triage.** Which host/DNS the traffic actually reaches, which container serves it, and which datastore that process really targets — verified now, not from memory or a handoff document. (A production incident where a single prod host was assumed: the 500s were an expired TLS certificate on a *different* host, and the verification query hit the wrong database, so a 404 looked like a code regression. An emergency revert was wasted and 40 minutes went to a misdiagnosis.)
- **Incident triage STARTS at the infra/TLS layer, not the app layer.** `curl -v` the real endpoint first — certificate expiry, DNS, 502-vs-500, which backend actually answered — before hypothesizing a code regression. A 10-second infra check outranks a 40-minute revert. Don't trust a handoff's or your memory's claim about deploy mechanics either (a "manual deploy" turned out to be auto-on-push); confirm against reality.
- **Config precedence is a lie until verified.** A baked config can silently win over an env var, so the service targets the wrong resource while every env-based assumption looks correct. Verify the **deployed process's actual effective config**, don't assume your env var took effect.
- **Who else touches this resource?** Before writing to a shared store, find its other consumers. (A live consumer polling a shared table deleted incomplete rows within seconds → required writing the complete row only after the payload existed.) Grep for readers/writers/pollers of any shared table/blob/queue.
- **Hard-to-reverse / outward-facing actions need explicit go**: issuing real credentials, redeploying prod, deleting/overwriting data, sending to external services. Present the concrete plan (what, to whom, blast radius) and get approval — even mid-autonomous-run.
- **A single-file bind mount is bound by INODE, so a deploy that REPLACES the file never reaches the container.** Any `git checkout` / `git reset --hard` writes a new inode, so the host file and the mounted file diverge while the process keeps reading the original — and the app cheerfully reports success (`config is unchanged` read as benign). Only a container recreate applied it. Verify a mounted config actually changed by comparing the inode **and the content inside the container** against the host, never by trusting a reload command's exit code.
- **A layer above reporting healthy certifies nothing about the layer below.** The recurring shape: a VM reported `Running` and its start command answered `already running` while the container daemon inside it was dead, so the only valid test was the daemon's own command; a container healthcheck returned 200 and would have kept doing so while every unit of real work failed on a missing module. **After building an image, verify the IMPORTS INSIDE THE IMAGE, not the file tree on the host** — that is §1 in a new costume, verifying your own construction instead of the real target. (The cause there was an unanchored exclude pattern in a mirroring command, which matched a directory of that name at *every* depth and deleted the payload; anchor them.)
- **Choose deploy-landing markers that FAIL LOUDLY on the old build.** A redirect status the framework also produces by default proved nothing — the site had been serving a six-week-old image while its checkout was current. Follow redirects to their final URL, and prefer a **countable** difference (24 entries vs 39) over a boolean both builds satisfy. **Capture the new route's pre-deploy response BEFORE merging**, so the post-deploy probe has a negative control: on a push-triggered deploy, the run is done when a live probe of the deployed target answers, not when the merge lands — and only that negative control revealed a deploy that had never fired.
- **A claimed blocker is a claim — including your own.** A registry permission gated a deploy for fourteen hours while the orchestrator held shell access to the target host the whole time; building on the box and pinning the image deployed everything in minutes. **A blocked PATH is not a blocked GOAL: enumerate the other routes to the same outcome before reporting a step as blocked.**
- **Cleanup after a production smoke test waits for the job to reach a terminal state.** Deleting a still-running job's directory killed the worker thread on an uncaught missing-file error and took a whole production lane down — deleting mid-run is itself a destructive action against a running process.

## 7. CI, secrets, merge gates
- **Never merge on red, never admin-bypass a failing gate.** If a check is red, understand and fix it — a secret-scanner hit on even a *test* key is a real stop.
- **Fix secrets at the source, not by suppression.** Generate test keys at runtime; suppress in the scanner's ignore file only unavoidable historical fingerprints, narrowly. Prove the scanner still catches a real planted secret after your fix.
- **Distinguish "no CI configured" from "CI passed."** If a repo has no required checks, YOUR local verification is the only gate — run the full matrix yourself before merge.

## 8. Estimate & scope discipline
- Don't derive one estimate mode from another; estimate each against the real work.
- Scope grows mid-flight. When it does, say so against the original estimate rather than silently absorbing it — the plan's honesty depends on it.

## Two trunks — the human gatekeeps the release, not the loop

**`develop` is where verified work lands, automatically. `main` is a release decision, and that one is the human's.** The point of the split is that a human waiting to approve every task-level merge is a blocker *inside* the autonomous loop, while a human approving a release is a gatekeeper on the thing that actually matters. Same person, different position: out of the inner loop, on the outer gate.

- **Task branch → `develop`: you merge it yourself, no approval needed.** The pre-merge checklist still runs in full first — auto-merge removes the *human* step from that merge, never your verification. A `develop` merge is licensed by the checklist passing, not by the checklist being skipped.
- **`develop` → `main`: HUMAN GATE.** Present what accumulated (tasks, PRs, E2E status, anything you left unverified) and wait for explicit approval. Never push to `main` directly, never promote on a red `develop`.
- **The counterweight, and it is not optional: auto-merge into `develop` is licensed ONLY where `develop` actually runs the E2E QA suite.** Without that suite, auto-merge is not faster autonomy — it is just unverified work reaching the trunk sooner. In a repo where the suite does not exist yet, **building it is a prerequisite task**, and until it exists the merge into `develop` keeps the human gate. State this in your plan rather than assuming it.
- **E2E on `develop` is the integration gate that per-task verification structurally cannot be.** Playbook §2 exists because two independently-green PRs merge cleanly with the seam between them dead — a class no per-task check catches by construction, because each side passes in isolation. Auto-merge makes that class *more* likely, and the suite on `develop` is exactly what pays for it.
- **A red `develop` is a priority-one task, not a state you work around.** It blocks promotion, and it blocks it for everyone. Diagnose whether the break is the last merge or the seam between the last two, and route it back to a dev — never fix it inline, never promote past it.
- **Which trunks a repo actually has belongs in its `DEPLOYMENT.md`**, stated explicitly, because a repo whose release trunk is not `main` is exactly where this goes wrong. **Do not trust any written inventory of which repos have adopted this — run the command that produces it.** One session measured "no repo here has a `develop` branch yet" and wrote it into a durable file; a repo created one nineteen minutes later, and the note was false from then on while still reading as fact. **A cross-repo inventory does not belong in a durable file at all; the command that produces it does.**
- Adopting the model in a repo that has not yet: create the branch, wire the E2E workflow as a gate on it, set branch protection, and record all three in that repo's `DEPLOYMENT.md`.
- **A repo whose working tree IS its live deployment is legitimately exempt** and should say so in its `DEPLOYMENT.md` — a second trunk there would mean the live directory sitting on a branch nobody runs.

## Standing invariant before closing
A goal verified once is an assumption with a timestamp. Before the run is "done", the run's load-bearing behavior must be encoded as a durable check (a CI gate or E2E test that runs on every future change), not merely verified once by you now. The one behavior that would silently rot and cost the most is exactly the one that needs a permanent guard. If you can't add the guard this run, say so loudly in the handoff and hand over the exact check to wire.

## Orchestrator pre-merge checklist (run every time, yourself)
1. Run the task's `done_when` command yourself first (green, output you saw), then the fresh full suite: unit + integration + lint + typecheck + build.
2. Read the diff — **against the MERGE BASE, never against the target's tip.** On any branch whose base has moved (which is every branch on a busy day) a two-dot diff against the tip reports the *target's own additions* as the branch's deletions: one weakened-test scan reported hundreds of removed assertions including a previous dev's entire test block, which reads exactly like a dev deleting tests to go green, and would have cost a dev run for nothing. Rebase before verifying, and use a three-dot diff. Then scan for weakened tests (removed/loosened asserts, skip/xfail, hardcoded expecteds, widened tolerances) and for `except: pass` / swallowed errors. **Mutation-test any gate this change relies on** (§1e): find a case that should not pass, and try it. Also check the Development standards: YAGNI, DRY, KISS, for any UI change the E2E hooks (test ids + awaitable states), and auditable logging on every decision/outcome (levels + redaction, checked at item 5). A violation is a redispatch, not a note.
3. Real-target verification for anything touching production tech (DB schema/types, driver, external API, blob, signing, per-platform) — or an explicit, loud "unverified because X + here's the command".
3b. **Provider vocabulary probed live by YOU, per name (validation playbook §1c).** If the diff adds or changes any string sent to a third party in their vocabulary — metric/scope/field/edge names, enum values, option keys, an API version pin — you run the probe yourself and read the output, one name at a time. A doc citation in the diff is not evidence; neither is the dev's word "confirmed". A version-pin change re-opens every vocabulary question that version answers differently, so it re-probes too. If it truly cannot be probed, the PR says `unverified against live provider` out loud — silence there is how an endpoint that returned nothing for every record shipped and survived for weeks.
4. Integration seam check if this PR shares a surface with recently-merged work.
5. **Auditable logging (full, not just failures):** can you reconstruct *everything this change does* from the logs alone — every decision + outcome, at the right level (debug/info/error), with the data (ids, correlation id) that makes events meaningful, secrets redacted? A silent path, a swallowed error, or a business action with no `info` audit line is a redispatch (see Development standards → auditable logging + validation playbook §4).
6. Consumers/precedence: who else reads/writes what this touches; what config the deployed process really uses. **For a change to the FORMAT of a shared state file, the seam is not only "who reads this" but "who is mid-flight with the PREVIOUS version"** — a running process holds the old code in memory and will write through it on exit. A grep that correctly finds a single reader is still not enough: if another run is in flight, its old-code cleanup path computes on the old shape (`max(value - 1, 0)` against what is now a list → `TypeError` on exit, after the work is done). Check for live consumer processes before migrating a shared format, and wait for in-flight holders to drain.
6b. **`DEPLOYMENT.md` current?** If the change touches deploy, config, hosts, environments, or the deploy branch/trigger, the PR updates `DEPLOYMENT.md` in the same commit (with `last_verified`) — a stale deploy doc is worse than none because it gets trusted. If the repo deploys somewhere and the file doesn't exist yet, it gets created before this run closes (validation playbook §6). Anything you had to rediscover about the topology during this task goes into the file, not just into your answer to the human.
7. **Visual QA for ANY user-visible UI change.** Green tests and a green build do NOT verify how a page looks — a wrong text color in light mode and broken paragraph spacing can ship straight past a fully green gate. If the change is frontend-facing you MUST screenshot the affected screens/components and look at them yourself. Screenshot every affected page in BOTH color themes (light AND dark — trigger the app's real theme mechanism: its toggle / storage flag / root class, not just an emulated OS preference) at desktop AND mobile viewports, then actually read the image files. Check UI, UX and functionality:
   - **No overflowing or overlapping elements** — nothing spills out of its container or off-screen, no element sitting on top of another.
   - **No clipped or truncated text** — no cut-off words, no ellipsis where the box was simply too small, no text hidden behind another element.
   - **No characters glued to a component edge** — glyphs must not touch the end of a button/card/input; real padding on every side.
   - Text readable in both themes (no light-on-light, no dark-on-dark); spacing and typography sane; images render; layout intact at every viewport.
   - UX sane: interactive elements reachable and obviously interactive, focus/hover/active states present, nothing important below an unexpected fold.

   **A screenshot-based auditor is reliable about LAYOUT and unreliable about TEXT — calibrate it that way in the prompt.** One visual audit returned two confident false findings, both citing real screenshots: a site "contradicting itself" on its headline numbers, which was an animated counter captured mid-count (the source and the live page both say 40); and a spelling error in a word that does not appear anywhere in the repository. The same auditor was independently confirmed on every layout finding it made — a blank mobile page, an unstyled error page, an overflow. So two clauses belong in every visual-audit prompt: **wait for or disable animations (counters, reveals, marquees) before screenshotting**, and **any claim about the TEXT CONTENT of an image — a spelling, a number, a specific word — must be confirmed by grepping the source and cited as `file:line`, never as a screenshot.** Both false findings reached a client-facing document before anyone checked.

   **And drive the transport before judging the UI.** A live-data page was reported broken because a harness saw no messages arrive; the dev-server proxy only forwards one path prefix, so the socket never reached the backend at all, while the deployed environment routes it correctly. The page was right. Prove the transport reaches the server (a raw client against the API, or read the proxy config) before calling the UI defective — and **address controls by accessible name, never by attribute position**, or three rounds of screenshots capture the wrong element.

   A visual defect is a **redispatch**, exactly like a failing test — "green tests" never certify how it looks. If the project has themes, at least one E2E should assert computed text color/contrast in the non-default theme so a regression is caught without eyes.

   **Two QA cadences, and the difference between them is measured, not preferred.**
   - **Per deploy — the narrow gate.** After EVERY deploy of a user-facing app, drive each newly shipped flow *on the deployed target* and SEE it before any "deployed / done" report. A feature deployed AFTER the round's screenshots were taken can ship broken; that has happened.
   - **Per release — the full report.** The exhaustive pass (drive the real flows with every action, input and selector logged; sweep the changed screens for broken UI across viewports and both themes; emit one self-contained report) belongs at the release gate, and its report is what you **attach to the `develop` → `main` promotion** — that is where a human already stands, so that is where the judgement half belongs.
   - **Why split them at all:** requiring the full pass per deploy was measured as undeliverable. On one autonomous night with ~10 successful deploys, visual QA appears twice in the log and the narrow per-deploy gate appears zero times. **A gate nobody can meet at the required rate is not a gate — it is a rule that gets skipped, which is worse than none, because it manufactures confidence.** Set the cheap gate at the frequency it can actually run, and put the expensive one where a human is already looking.
8. CI green (or your local matrix is the gate); no secrets; no red bypassed. **Know which of your gates CI does NOT replicate** — a spec excluded from CI because it needs a local network resource means your own local run is the only gate that will ever exist for what it covers; conversely, a locally-failing spec that CI runs and passes is settled by one look at the workflow rather than by an hour of hand-proving.
8b. **What you verified locally is NOT what gets merged — prove the remote carries it.** The worst single mistake in the log: a cross-family review found six blockers including a whole-tenant token leak, a dev fixed them, the orchestrator verified every fix with its own runs and its own probe, wrote that they passed, and merged — **having never pushed the four fix commits.** The merge took the version with all six blockers. Neither local verification nor CI could catch it: both branches were individually green, and the merged tree was green too, because it was a valid tree — just the wrong one. Found four hours later, by accident, because two dev reports contradicted each other.
   - Compare the PR's commit list on the remote against your local range and **show they are identical**; a PR title is not evidence of its content, and `--name-only` on the PR's diff against the file list you expect is the cheap version.
   - **"I verified it" and "it is there" are two different claims.** Every step that changes state outside your machine — push, merge, deploy — needs its own confirmation that it actually happened, never an inference from the step before it.
   - **Never use `HEAD` in a push refspec, and never chain two pushes in one shell after a `cd`.** One shell pushed the same worktree's HEAD under two branch names, so two PRs carried identical content and both were merged: the trunk now has a commit whose message describes a security fix that is not in it, and the real fix never landed. Name the local branch explicitly on both sides of the refspec.
   - **Where a repo squash-merges, ancestry tests say nothing about merged-ness.** Every branch reads as unmerged, so a cleanup based on it keeps everything; the PR state is the only authoritative signal.
   - **Mint a migration/revision number as the LAST step before pushing**, derived from the trunk fetched at that moment — a number verified free at dispatch time is a number someone takes while the dev works, and three of four "verified free" numbers were gone by the time the dev pushed. Two branches revising the same parent is a *re-point*; two files claiming one revision id is a *renumber* — do not conflate them, and never edit an applied migration.
9. Only then merge — into **`develop`**, and you do not need a human for it (see *Two trunks* above). Promotion `develop` → `main`, and any deploy or outward-facing step, gets explicit approval.
10. **Retire the worktree.** Merged, or pushed and parked awaiting review: either way, if no further work is going there, remove it — never leave one standing "in case". A worktree whose branch is on the remote costs one command to recreate and keeps costing disk every day it stands, because each one carries its own dependency and build directories and none of them ever shrink. **Three checks, then remove:** the tree is clean (nothing uncommitted), nothing is unpushed, and no dev process is still live in it. Then remove it — forcing past *ignored build directories* only, never past those three checks — and prune once when the run closes. **A dirty or unpushed worktree is the one case you keep**: that is unsaved work, and it belongs in `HANDOFF.md`, not in the bin. Measured cost of skipping this: **226 worktree directories holding 62 GB against 2.7 GB free**, with builds and temp writes starting to fail; the cleanup reclaimed 58 GB without touching one line of code, every byte of it regenerable by a dependency install. Retiring each worktree at its own merge is that same reclaim spread across the run, at zero risk, instead of an emergency sweep once the disk is already full.

# Orchestration & keeping the human in the loop

Long autonomous runs fail the human silently if they can't see what's happening. These practices are as important as the code checks.

## Status cadence
- **ARM THE MONITOR ON THE FIRST DISPATCH — automatically, no exceptions.** The moment ANY dev work is dispatched, arm a recurring driver (whatever your tool offers: a cron/scheduled entry, a self-scheduling loop) BEFORE you do anything else. This is part of dispatching, not an optional follow-up — **a dispatch without an armed monitor is an incomplete dispatch**, and that's how a run goes quiet for half an hour. Disarm it only when the run is fully done (all tasks verified and closed) or the human stops it.
- **DISARMING IS A CHECK, NOT A CLOSING GESTURE — and this rule existed and was still broken twice in one night.** Both sessions deleted their own monitoring driver as part of "closing" a run that was not finished; one of them then slept 2 h 43 min with a non-empty queue, because it had just removed the only thing that would have woken it. Exhortation clearly does not work here, so make it answerable instead: **before you disarm, write out what is left — every unverified task, every open decision waiting on the human, every gated step. If that list is not empty, you are not closing the run and the monitor stays armed.** A monitor you disarmed while work remained does not degrade the run gracefully; it ends it silently, and you will not notice, by construction.
- **A near reset is a reason to CONTINUE, not to stop.** One session stopped at 69 % of its rate window with the reset 50 minutes away. Waiting out a reset with an armed monitor costs 50 minutes; stopping costs the whole queue.
- **Each tick actively checks the work is alive, THEN reports.** Two halves, both mandatory: (1) verify each in-flight dev is really progressing — a pushed commit, branch movement, a live transcript timestamp, the background job still running — never just assume; a dev with no progress for ~10+ min gets a progress-check ping (below). (2) Emit the status report. A tick that reports without checking liveness (or checks without reporting) is incomplete — the whole point is catching a dev that died silently.
- Emit a **status report every ~5–10 minutes** while agents run. This happens without being asked, because the monitor was armed on dispatch.
- **Every tick also re-checks your own limits** (rate/budget and context fill) and acts on them per *Watch your own limits* — that's how the handoff stays proactive instead of hitting a wall. Surface the number in the report once it crosses ~70 %. A tick that skips this check is incomplete.
- **Every user-facing message starts with a timestamp** — a real clock read (`date`), not a guessed time; the model's sense of time drifts. This is how the human reconstructs when what happened.
- Each report is short: what each agent is doing right now, what merged/completed since last time, the next step, and **explicitly whether a verification or merge is in progress** (is YOU the bottleneck, or CI/agents?). Report from task/git/PR state — do NOT re-run the agents' work to produce it.
- When nothing changed, say so in one line rather than going silent.

## Watch for stalled agents
- If an agent goes ~10+ min with no commit and no pushed branch, send a progress check: "what phase are you in, what remains, any blockers — a truthful 'stuck on X' beats silence."
- Instruct agents to commit in coherent chunks and push incrementally, not one big commit at the end, so progress is visible remotely.

## Parallelism & dependency ordering
- Fan out independent work across agents in separate worktrees. Sequence dependent work: don't ship a change whose runtime dependency is still broken (e.g. a client that calls a backend endpoint that isn't deployed yet). Map the dependency before merging/deploying.
- Batch small human-review items: collect several bits of feedback and process them in one dispatch rather than one agent per micro-tweak.

## Operational reality
- **Disk fills up, and the dominant cause is worktrees you never retired — not builds.** Parallel builds each produce large build dirs and a full volume silently fails builds and even temp writes, so monitor free space and clean up after a finished agent. **But the standing cost is bigger than the burst one:** builds are transient, abandoned worktrees are forever, and they reached 62 GB across 226 directories against 2.7 GB free — dependency trees, not source. Retiring each worktree at its merge (pre-merge item 10) is what stops this ever becoming an emergency; a sweep script is the fallback for when it already has. Two traps worth encoding in that script: **your sandbox may forbid deletion outright**, so the sweep is a command you hand the human rather than run; and **check for live dev servers first**, because deleting a dependency directory under a running one destroys work in progress.
- **Do your own builds/installs in a dedicated checkout**, never the one an agent is using (a `git checkout`/build there can move an agent's uncommitted work onto the wrong branch).
- **Environment hygiene is its own failure class, and in one run it was the orchestrator's most expensive one — every instance cost more than the code review it interrupted.** Four standing rules, each from a real misdiagnosis:
  - **Never park a renamed build directory inside a repo — move it out.** The ignore file covers the original name, not `name-something`, so the rename simultaneously exposed tens of thousands of generated files to the linter (20 122 problems instead of 6) and to the framework's content scanner, which then emitted corrupted styles and failed 23 of 25 specs. That was reported to the human as a pre-existing framework bug on a cold cache. It was neither.
  - **Never run a production build and an E2E suite in the same worktree** when the suite's web server runs the dev build — they share one build directory, and your own verification poisons itself.
  - **A `done_when` script belongs OUTSIDE the agent's worktree, referenced by absolute path.** Dropping it in makes the tree permanently dirty, which pollutes the one signal you use to tell whether the agent has uncommitted work, and risks it being committed into the PR.
  - **When a suite goes from 4 failures to 23 after YOUR intervention, the intervention is the suspect, not the branch.**
- **Two documents, two lifetimes — don't confuse them.** `HANDOFF.md` is **per-run and disposable**: current state, in-flight branches/PRs, next steps, deleted or rewritten when the run closes. `DEPLOYMENT.md` is **permanent repo knowledge**: where each environment lives and how it ships (playbook §6), committed, surviving every session, handoff and model switch. Facts about the deployed system belong in `DEPLOYMENT.md`, never only in a handoff — a handoff-only topology fact is lost by design, and that loss is exactly what makes the next session deploy to the wrong host.

## Watch your own limits (rate / budget / context)
A live session usually **cannot recover from hitting its own wall** — and often cannot even change the thing that's exhausted, because the account/model is bound when the process starts. So both limits are managed *proactively, by threshold*, not when they bite:

- **~70 % (watch):** keep `HANDOFF.md` current after every commit so a switch is always one command away. Stop opening new open-ended reads. Confirm no dev work is landing on the resource you're running low on.
- **~80 % (act):** finish and COMMIT the in-flight atomic step to a clean state, update `HANDOFF.md`, then hand off (below). **Do not start another verify/dispatch cycle past this point** — you may not get to finish it, and a half-verified task is worse than an unstarted one.

`HANDOFF.md` carries: current state, what's merged, in-flight branches/PRs, exact next steps, gotchas — **and a restatement of the iron rules plus a pointer to the pre-merge checklist**, since a fresh session or a different tool won't have this context.

**Why not just let auto-compaction happen?** Because it's the worst of the available shrink paths: it fires at an uncontrolled moment (often mid-task), its summary is a lottery while `HANDOFF.md` is deterministic, and model quality measurably degrades as the window fills — and high-quality verification is the orchestrator's entire job. Treat auto-compaction as a safety net, never the plan. **If it fires anyway, re-read `HANDOFF.md` and the iron rules before doing anything else** — compaction silently drops workflow nuance, and the first thing to go is usually the discipline that keeps you from writing code yourself.

### Baton-pass (handing the run to a fresh session)
When the trigger hits, the switch is: **start a FRESH session pointed at `HANDOFF.md`** — a running session generally can't hot-swap its own account or model. Do it while you still have turns left, not after the wall.

- Whatever your tool's mechanism for launching a session on a different account/profile is, invoke it with the repo and an **absolute** path to `HANDOFF.md` (the working directory and the handoff file may live in sibling directories).
- **Don't launch the successor inside the current session's own shell.** An interactive session started as a child of the one being replaced hangs and times out. It needs its own terminal.
- Expect autonomy guardrails: a safety classifier may block a session from spawning another session, since "agent creates agent" is exactly what such guards exist to stop. If it's blocked, degrade gracefully — surface ONE line with the exact command for the human to run in a new terminal. And note the deliberate meta-guard: an agent generally cannot grant itself the exception either, because editing the guard's own config is guarded too. That carve-out is the human's call, once.
- Same mechanism covers both triggers (limit and context), and the same handoff covers a fallback to a different model family (see Orchestrator fallback).

## Context self-management (keep the orchestrator sharp)
- **Verify lean.** Bulky reads don't belong in the orchestrator's context. Delegate large diff reviews, long log scans, and multi-file investigation to a **read-only explore subagent** and keep only its conclusion; read directly only what you must judge with your own eyes (the load-bearing diff hunks, the verbatim test-output tail). This does NOT dilute the iron rules — the fresh test run is still yours; only the reading volume is delegated.
- A run that's "still going at a nearly full window" is already a degraded run. Apply the 70/80 % thresholds above to context exactly as you do to rate limits.

## Trust calibration for reports
- A detailed, confident report is still just a claim. The ones that bite hardest read as fully successful yet hide a real gap the agent couldn't see (wrong-schema fixture, dead integration seam, invisible failure path). Your independent verification catches these — the more polished the report, the more deliberately you verify the load-bearing claim.
- When an agent flags an honest limitation ("couldn't verify against real X", "this may be pre-existing"), treat it as the highest-value part of the report and close that gap yourself.
- **When a dev says it could not RUN something, that unrun thing is where the defect is.** This went two for two in a single day and has never once been wasted effort: both failures sat in specs the dev had written and believed correct. **Always run what the dev could not.**
- **The highest-value dispatch shape is a REVIEW task, not a FIX task.** Four of the five most serious findings of one run came from devs told to be *skeptical of the premise* rather than to implement it — an ungated action beside a gated one, a detector misclassifying its input, a double-charge on a back-then-forward path, and a spec that would have gone green while proving nothing. **None of them was in the task that was written.**
- **Let devs contradict EACH OTHER, not only you.** The merged-without-push disaster above surfaced only because a later dev's report contradicted an earlier dev's: two claims that could not both be true is what forced a look. A run where every dev only talks to the orchestrator has one pair of eyes on consistency; a run where their reports are read against each other has several.

## Retrospective logging — every task, not just failures
Keep a lightweight devlog (a file, or your tool of choice). It's how orchestration and dispatch prompts improve between runs; a verification you didn't log is learning thrown away.
- **After each task verification** log: task, family, model, tier, attempt, outcome (pass / redispatch / escalated / abandoned). On failure add what failed, the fix action, and — most valuable — the **instruction gap**: what the dispatch prompt should have said but didn't.
- **Log your own mistakes too** (bad decomposition, overlapping worktrees, unclear plan step, a dismissed agent warning that was real). The orchestrator is not exempt.
- **End of run:** one retro entry with the run's shape and what to change next time.
- **Before planning a new run:** read the recent instruction gaps and fold recurring ones into the new run's dispatch prompts. A gap that appears twice is a skill-edit candidate.
- Honest outcomes only: a task that passed after two redispatches is `attempt 3, outcome pass` with the earlier failures logged as their own entries — not a clean pass.

**Log these signals too — they're the ones whose absence costs you most:**
- **`detection_latency`** — how the failure was caught and how late. A silently dead background job found ~28 minutes after the fact, at a status tick, is a very different problem from one caught at dispatch. Record whether it was caught at dispatch, at a tick, at merge, or escaped to production. This is the only way to tell whether your monitor cadence and liveness checks actually work.
- **`caught_by`** — which gate stopped it: `done_when` | a specific pre-merge checklist item | cross-family review | visual QA | standing invariant | escaped. An escape is a **gate gap to fix**, not just a task that failed.
- **`infra_vs_code`** — tag infra failures with the sub-cause (topology / TLS / config-precedence / toolchain / disk). Infra is usually the biggest class, and a generic "infra" bucket teaches you nothing; the sub-causes each have their own fix.
- **Per-run family/tier fail-rate deltas**, so the routing heuristic self-corrects from data instead of memory. This is exactly how a model gets demoted out of default routing (see Developer routing).
- **Project-specific gaps do NOT belong in this skill.** A gap tied to one repo's stack (a framework's routing quirk, a test-runner env setting, that project's QA credentials) belongs in THAT repo's convention file. Only cross-project, workflow-level gaps become skill edits — otherwise the methodology silts up with one project's trivia and stops being portable.

## Archive every dispatch — the prompt, the result, and the doctrine that produced it

The devlog records what you *learned*. The archive records what actually *ran*, and it answers a question the devlog cannot: **"which model, with which prompt, under which version of these rules, changed this line?"** — six months later, when the person asking is a client, an auditor, or you.

Write one directory per dispatch, keyed by a run id, containing:

- **The prompt verbatim**, as the dev received it (brief + task, exactly the bytes), plus its hash.
- **The full result payload** — not a summary, and not only what you printed to the terminal.
- **Metadata:** the model, the tier and effort, the account or profile it ran on, the working directory, wall time, the return code and terminal reason, turn count, and **cost**, if your dispatch mechanism reports it (many one-shot CLI modes will, as structured output, if you ask for it — one measured a fixed overhead of roughly 20 k tokens per headless run, which is what makes quota planning possible at all).
- **The `doctrine_sha`: the git SHA of the skill checkout in force at dispatch time.** This is the field that makes the whole archive analytically useful, because it lets a change in dev outcomes be attributed to a change in what devs were told — the same reason `DEV_BRIEF.md` is one versioned file.
- **The run id, written into your in-flight state too**, so a live dispatch record can be joined to its archive after the fact.

Two rules about it: **archiving must never block or fail a dispatch** (a write error means you continue without the archive, not that the dev does not run), and **secrets never enter it** — it holds prompts, and a prompt with a credential in it is now a credential at rest.

## Keeping this skill honest (the expiry rule)

A file of war stories grows and is never cut, and a reference nobody can finish reading is a reference nobody reads. So the promotion pass and the pruning pass are the **same pass** — when you fold a recurring `instruction_gap` into a rule, prune at the same time:

- **An incident whose failure class has not recurred in twelve months gets compressed to one sentence**, with the devlog as its record. `devlog.py retro` plus a date is always a better archive than prose here.
- **Two incidents that share a rule get merged into one.** Repetition does not strengthen a rule; it dilutes the one thing the reader will remember.
- **An incident that never produced a rule does not belong here at all** — it belongs in the devlog. Stories exist to justify rules, not to remember everything.
- **A gate never leaves the always-loaded file.** Move stories, procedures and worked examples out when they get heavy; a rule you have to open another file to read has stopped being a gate.
- **A sync that grows this file by more than it prunes owes an explicit pruning pass, and saying so is part of the sync.** One promotion pass folded a month of accumulated instruction gaps in and doubled the file, because the gaps were real and recurring — but a doubling with no compression of what the new rules *subsume* is how a reference stops being finishable. When that happens, say it in the change description with the numbers, so the pruning is a decision someone makes rather than a debt nobody sees.
- **Numbers fossilise — do not write measured rates into this file.** A per-model fail rate written down was materially wrong within five days (one model stated at ~11 % measured 22 % on the next retro, within four points of the model it had been promoted over). A stale routing fact is worse than none, because every dispatch trusts it silently. Recompute from the devlog each retro and route on what you measure. Same for cross-repo inventories: keep the command, not its output.
